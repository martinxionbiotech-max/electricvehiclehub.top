# EU Homologation: Making a Chinese EV Road-Legal

Taxation gets an EV *into* the EU. Homologation is what makes it legal to *drive*. These are separate problems, and conflating them is one of the most common — and most costly — mistakes a first-time importer makes.

## The three approval routes

The EU offers three broad paths to make an imported vehicle road-legal, distinguished mainly by volume.

### 1. EU Whole Vehicle Type Approval (WVTA)

The standard route for manufacturers importing at scale. A WVTA certifies that a vehicle type meets all applicable EU technical requirements across the single market. It is expensive and process-heavy, and generally only makes sense for a manufacturer or a large-volume importer.

### 2. Small Series Type Approval

For importers bringing in a limited annual volume (the exact threshold varies by vehicle category), a Small Series approval relaxes some requirements. It is the practical middle ground for specialist importers.

### 3. National single-vehicle approval

For individual vehicles, each EU member state offers a route to approve a single car against adapted requirements:

- **Germany:** §21 StVZO approval via a technical inspection organisation (TÜV, DEKRA, GTÜ).
- **Netherlands, France, and others:** each has an analogous single-vehicle procedure.

This is the most common route for private imports and low-volume dealer imports, because it is far cheaper than full type approval.

## What a China-market EV typically fails

A vehicle built for the Chinese domestic market is not automatically compliant in the EU. Common gaps include:

- **Lighting conformity** — headlamp beam pattern and light signatures may not meet EU requirements.
- **Speedometer and display** — must show km/h in the correct format; Chinese-market displays may differ.
- **Electrical and safety standards** — charging port interlock, safety systems, and homologated component conformity.
- **Noise and environmental** — the EU requires compliance with relevant acoustic and environmental standards.

Each gap is either a documentation exercise or a physical modification, and the physical ones are where cost and time accumulate.

## Cost and timeline

For a small-volume or single-vehicle import, budget:

- **Cost:** $1,500–$5,000, depending on the vehicle and the extent of adaptation.
- **Timeline:** 2–6 weeks, longer if parts need to be sourced.

Don't shortcut this step. A vehicle that fails its first approval attempt costs more in re-testing and rework than doing it correctly the first time.
