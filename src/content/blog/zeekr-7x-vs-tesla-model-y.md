## TL;DR

The [Zeekr 7X](/vehicles/zeekr-7x/) is the closest dimensional match the Model Y has faced in this comparison series — 4,825 mm against 4,797 mm — and its landed-cost map splits almost evenly: **$56,938.33 in Germany (+75.9%), $11,065 above a Berlin-built Model Y from €39,990**, then **$7,177, $7,511, $12,401 and $485 below the Model Y's local entry in the UK, the UAE, Thailand and Australia**. Zeekr pays the **18.8% countervailing rate it inherits from Geely** — and the arithmetic says the rate is not what decides Germany: rebuilt at Tesla's own 7.8%, the same stack still lands ≈$6,404 above the Model Y.

## The basis: ex-factory versus landed cost

**Ex-factory** is the China gate price — $32,370 for the [7X](/vehicles/zeekr-7x/). **Landed cost** is what an importer pays to get that car through customs and onto the road in the destination market: standard duty on the CIF value, countervailing duty on the duty-paid value, VAT or GST compounding last, plus a fixed block for RoRo freight, clearance, certification, registration and inland transport. It is cash at the border — not a retail price, and not inclusive of importer margin or warranty provisioning. The compounding chain is documented in the [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/); why we compare cost against sticker has [its own explainer](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/).

The asymmetry from this series carries over: the Model Y column is a **local retail price** with Tesla's taxes, logistics and margin already inside; the 7X column is an import-cost estimate with no margin. That frame answers the question this article exists for: how much headroom does an importer have against the shelf price customers compare against?

Two cautions, both familiar from this series. Second, where a Model Y figure is not published for the entry trim in the sources below, the table says so rather than estimating it.

The honest read: the 7X is the bigger, heavier car — 359 kg more than the entry Model Y — with the larger quoted battery and nearly twice the quoted motor output. Whether that wins on the road turns on efficiency at speed, the charging curve and service coverage, none of which a spec sheet settles. On paper, it is the most literal product answer to the Model Y this comparison series has priced.

## The tariff wall: one inherited rate, one avoided border

Both cars are China-related for tariff purposes. That sentence does very different work in different jurisdictions:

| Layer | 7X into Germany | Model Y for Germany |
|---|---|---|
| Standard import duty | 10% | None — Berlin-built; no customs entry |
| Countervailing duty | **18.8%** (Geely group, individually sampled) | None — outside the measure |
| VAT | 19% compounded over both | 19% (inside retail price) |

The 18.8% is not a Zeekr finding — it is Geely's sampled rate travelling with the group, the way it does for Lynk & Co and Galaxy ([the full explainer](/blog/zeekr-countervailing-duty/), [the group's rate story](/blog/geely-188-countervailing-duty/)). It is the second-lowest rate the EU assigned to any Chinese auto group, after BYD's 17.0%, and it sits 1.9 points kinder than the 20.7% that most of the field, including XPeng and Xiaomi, pays ([master table](/blog/china-ev-brand-cvd-master-table/)). That is the best tariff card a non-BYD premium challenger has been dealt.

None of it touches the German duel, because the car on the other side never crosses a customs border. The Model Y sold in Europe is built at Giga Berlin; when Tesla does export China-built cars — the Model 3 volume for Europe, Model Y units for several non-EU markets — they pay Tesla's individually assessed 7.8%. The 7X pays 10% + 18.8% + 19%, compounded, on a car designed to meet the Y head-on. That stack, against a border-free rival, sets up the German number below.

## Worked example: the 7X through German customs

The German stack, line by line, under the site's [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/) — standard duty first, countervailing duty on the duty-paid value, VAT last, then the fixed block:

| Line (Germany) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (China ex-factory) | $32,370.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $3,237.00 |
| Countervailing duty (18.8%) | $6,694.12 |
| VAT (19%) | $8,037.21 |
| RoRo freight | $2,000.00 |
| Customs clearance | $350.00 |
| Certification / homologation | $3,250.00 |
| Registration | $500.00 |
| Inland transport | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$56,938.33** |

Formula: `32,370 × 1.10 × 1.188 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 56,938.33`. That matches the Germany figure in the 7X vehicle record to the cent. Note the shape of the stack: the countervailing line — **$6,694.12** — is 2.07× the standard duty and larger than the entire freight-and-compliance block ($6,600) that gets the car to the customer.

### The counterfactual: one car, three rates

A rate is assigned by regulation, not selected by the importer. But rebuilding the same stack at other rates locates exactly where the German problem lives — and how much of it a rate change could ever fix:

| German stack for the 7X | CVD rate | CVD $ | Total landed | vs Model Y entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| As shipped — Geely group rate | 18.8% | $6,694.12 | $56,938.33 | −$11,065 (7X above) |
| At Tesla's assessed China rate | 7.8% | $2,777.35 | $52,277.37 | −$6,404 (7X above) |
| At the "other cooperating" default | 20.7% | $7,370.65 | $57,743.40 | −$11,870 (7X above) |

Formulas: `32,370 × 1.10 × 1.078 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 52,277.37` and `32,370 × 1.10 × 1.207 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 57,743.40`.

Reading the table: the inherited rate is a real but small perk — ≈$805 landed against what the default rate would cost. The 11-point gap to Tesla's own rate is the big line, **$4,660.96** per car. And even deleted entirely, ≈**$6,404** of German deficit remains. Unlike the cheaper G6 — where the same rate swap put the car below the German entry in our earlier comparison — no countervailing-duty rate puts the 7X under the German sticker. Its German case has to be a product case.

## Why the same car flips from $11,065 behind to $12,401 ahead

- **Inside the EU, the stack compounds against a border-free rival.** Germany (−$11,065), France (−$10,241) and Spain (−$9,250) are the losing lanes: 10% + 18.8% + VAT lands on a $32,370 base while the competing car pays no import line at all.
- **Norway is the narrowest loss — $2,927.** Zero import duty, but 25% VAT on the full base against a keenly priced NOK 422,560 entry; the one losing market where a couple of points of importer discipline could close the gap.
- **The wins are real and clustered outside the customs wall.** UK +$7,177 (10% duty, no CVD, 20% VAT), UAE +$7,511 (5% + 5%), Thailand +$12,401 (0% + 7%) and Australia +$485 (0% + 10%, essentially level against a sticker that excludes on-road costs).
- **Base price beats rate inside the challenger pack.** The 7X pays 1.9 points less countervailing duty than the G6 (18.8% vs 20.7%) yet lands further above the German entry, because its ex-factory base sits $7,540 higher. The invoice, not the tariff line, is the German variable.

## Where this comparison can go wrong

1. **Cost versus sticker.** The 7X column is cash at the border; the Model Y column is a retail product with a service network included. The subtraction measures headroom, not value — [sticker vs landed](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/) covers the frame.
2. **VAT is not always a cost.** Registered B2B importers usually recover VAT, which cuts the cash needed at the border. Duty and countervailing duty never come back; the German ranking survives either way.
3. **Fixed costs and FX are central estimates.** Freight, certification and clearance are volume-sensitive; conversions use 22 September 2026 rates, and a 2% move in the euro shifts the German comparison by roughly $900.
4. **Range cycles are not interchangeable.** 620 km CLTC cannot be subtracted from 534 km WLTP; the [cycle explainer](/blog/cltc-vs-wltp-range/) covers the mechanics.
5. **Trim variance and moving prices.** Model Y trims and packs differ by market, and Tesla prices have moved this year — the French entry rose €1,000 in April 2026 and the German cash price sits at €40,970. Re-quote before committing.
6. **Duty regimes are not furniture.** The EU countervailing schedule is under review and legal challenge; stress any deal a few points in either direction and re-check the rate quarterly.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view, the 7X is the most literal Model Y answer in this series — 28 mm of length and 35 mm of wheelbase apart — and its landed-cost map is the cleanest split these duels have produced: four wins, four losses, decided almost entirely by whether the destination sits inside the EU's customs wall. Geely's inherited rate is the best tariff card a non-BYD premium challenger has been dealt; it is still not the reason to buy or skip this car. My read: build the case in the UK, the UAE and Thailand, where the 7X lands $7,177–$12,401 under Tesla's local entry, and treat Germany, France and Spain as specification plays, because no realistic rate change rescues the arithmetic there.

### Reasoning

First, the decomposition: of the $11,065 German deficit, $4,660.96 is the 11-point spread between Geely's 18.8% and Tesla's 7.8% — a rate no regulator is going to hand Zeekr, since it applies to Tesla's own China exports. Remove it as an arithmetic exercise and $6,404 of deficit still stands, held up by base price, the fixed block and the VAT layer. That tells an importer where to spend negotiating energy: on the specification and trim mix, not at the border.

Second, the map is consistent with this series and clearer than most: the 7X loses its three EU lanes plus Norway, and wins every non-EU lane. Same car, same freight — the flip is customs geography, and it says the market decision precedes the product decision.

Third, the challenger-pack comparison is counterintuitive in a useful way: the 7X carries a better rate than the G6 (18.8% vs 20.7%) and a worse one than nothing at all, and it lands between the G6's roughly $1,120 German deficit and the YU7's $17,132 — because base price, not the tariff line, does the heavy lifting. Read the invoice before the regulation.

Fourth, Norway is the tell for how thin a non-EU edge can get: zero duty, but 25% VAT and a keen local price leave a $2,927 deficit — narrower than any EU lane, yet still a deficit. Real headroom exists only where the Model Y's local price is high and the border is light: the UK, the UAE and Thailand. That is where a 7X business should be built first.
