## TL;DR

Both cars chase the same importer shortlist, and the landed-cost gap between them runs **$6,078–$9,293** across seven markets: the Zeekr X lands at **$40,719 in Germany, $35,211 in the UK and $28,639 in the UAE**, against **$31,503, $27,713 and $22,377** for the BYD Atto 3 in the same three markets. The spread is set by ex-factory price and producer-specific EU countervailing duties rather than size — the two cars are within 5 mm of each other in length.

## Key statistics

- **Zeekr X base price:** $21,940 China ex-factory — 61.47 kWh battery, 530 km CLTC, 250 kW
- **BYD Atto 3 base price:** $16,260 China ex-factory — 49.92 kWh Blade battery, 430 km CLTC, 150 kW
- **Landed in Germany:** Zeekr X $40,719 (+85.6%) vs Atto 3 $31,503 (+93.7%)
- **Landed in the UK:** $35,211 (+60.5%) vs $27,713 (+70.4%)
- **Landed in the UAE:** $28,639 (+30.5%) vs $22,377 (+37.6%)
- **Gap across the seven markets here:** $6,078 (Thailand, narrowest) to $9,293 (France, widest)
- **Why it moves:** EU countervailing duties — 18.8% for Zeekr (Geely group), 17.0% for BYD — compound with 10% duty and 19–20% VAT inside the EU; the UK, Norway, Australia, the UAE and Thailand add no countervailing duty at all
- **The counterintuitive part:** the cheaper car carries the higher percentage premium in every market, because the fixed import block spreads over a smaller base

## The basis: ex-factory versus landed cost

Plain language first. **Ex-factory** is the price at the factory gate in China — what the car costs before it moves anywhere. Both figures in this comparison are China ex-factory MSRP from the EV Hub catalog: $21,940 for the [Zeekr X](/vehicles/zeekr-x/), $16,260 for the [BYD Atto 3](/vehicles/byd-atto-3/) (sold as the Yuan Plus in China).

**Landed cost** is what an importer pays to get that car through customs in the destination market: the ex-factory price compounded through standard import duty, countervailing duty where the EU applies it, and VAT or GST — plus a fixed block covering RoRo freight, customs clearance, certification, registration and inland transport. It is the cash needed at the border, not the price the car sells for; no importer margin, dealer spread or warranty provisioning is included.

The arithmetic compounds rather than adds:

```
landed = base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs
```

Adding those rates instead of compounding them is the most common — and most expensive — error in import math. The worked basis, including where the EU's producer-specific duties come from, sits in EV Hub's [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/). One practical note from that page: VAT is often recoverable for registered B2B importers, so treat the VAT line as cash flow at the border rather than a final cost — duty and countervailing duty never come back.

## The gap, market by market

| Market | Zeekr X landed | Atto 3 landed | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | $40,719 | $31,503 | $9,216 |
| France | $40,905 | $31,612 | $9,293 |
| United Kingdom | $35,211 | $27,713 | $7,498 |
| Norway | $34,675 | $27,575 | $7,100 |
| Australia | $29,984 | $23,736 | $6,248 |
| United Arab Emirates | $28,639 | $22,377 | $6,262 |
| Thailand | $28,126 | $22,048 | $6,078 |

Why the gap moves:

- **EU markets carry the widest gaps.** France $9,293 and Germany $9,216 — 10% duty, a producer-specific countervailing duty on the duty-paid value, then 19–20% VAT compounding on top. Differences in base price and CVD rate amplify each other.
- **No-CVD markets narrow the spread.** The UK sits at $7,498 (10% duty, 20% VAT, no CVD); Norway at $7,100 (zero duty, 25% VAT, no CVD) — it lands cheaper than the UK despite the higher VAT rate because no duty applies at all.
- **The cheapest lanes are the low-tax lanes.** Australia $6,248 (zero duty, 10% GST), the UAE $6,262 (5% duty, 5% VAT) and Thailand $6,078, the narrowest gap in the set (zero duty, 7% VAT).
- **Percentages flip the story.** The Atto 3 wears the heavier percentage premium in all seven markets — +93.7% against +85.6% in Germany, +35.6% against +28.2% in Thailand — because the same fixed block weighs more heavily on a smaller base. Read the absolute dollars for decisions; the percentages describe burden, not price.

## Worked example: both cars through the same German customs office

| Line (Germany) | Zeekr X | BYD Atto 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Base price (China ex-factory) | $21,940.00 | $16,260.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $2,194.00 | $1,626.00 |
| Countervailing duty (18.8% / 17.0%) | $4,537.19 | $3,040.62 |
| VAT (19%) | $5,447.53 | $3,976.06 |
| RoRo freight | $2,000.00 | $2,000.00 |
| Customs clearance | $350.00 | $350.00 |
| Certification / homologation | $3,250.00 | $3,250.00 |
| Registration | $500.00 | $500.00 |
| Inland transport | $500.00 | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$40,718.72** | **$31,502.68** |

The formulas, in order: `21,940 × 1.10 × 1.188 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 40,718.72` and `16,260 × 1.10 × 1.17 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 31,502.68`. Of the $9,216.04 gap between the two German totals, $5,680.00 is the base-price difference and $3,536.04 is compounded duty and VAT — the countervailing-duty line alone contributes $1,496.57, because the more expensive car also pays the higher CVD rate.

## Where this comparison can go wrong

1. **Cost versus sticker.** Both landed columns are import costs, not retail prices — no margin, warranty reserve or dealer spread is inside. Subtracting one from a showroom price measures the wrong thing; [why the sticker price is never the landed price](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/) covers that error properly.
2. **VAT is not always a cost.** Registered B2B importers usually recover VAT or GST, which cuts the border cash requirement rather than the underlying cost. Duty and CVD are permanent; the gap stays either way.
3. **Fixed costs are central estimates.** They are volume- and lane-sensitive; per-unit freight and certification fall with scale, and small orders land above these numbers.
4. **Quoted specs, not measured results.** The range and charging figures are catalog specifications on the CLTC cycle; they are comparable between these two cars and not comparable to WLTP or real-world distances.
5. **Duty regimes move.** The EU's countervailing-duty regime is under periodic review and legal challenge; non-EU rates can change too. Model at current rates, then stress the deal at a few points in either direction.
6. **Keep the comparisons separate.** This pair is one table; the Atto 3's landed position against a mid-size Tesla Model Y is a different segment exercise altogether — [that comparison lives here](/blog/byd-atto-3-vs-tesla-model-y/). Do not net the two tables together.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view, the Zeekr X–Atto 3 gap is the most stable number in Chinese-EV importing right now. It holds in every market modelled here — $6,078 at its narrowest, $9,293 at its widest — because both cars pay the same fixed block on arrival and most of the difference lives at the factory gate. For an importer, that stability is the point: this is a tier decision you can underwrite, not a tariff arbitrage that appears and disappears with a policy review. If the buyer needs the extra range and pace, the X's premium is predictable; if the deal needs the lowest cash at the border, the Atto 3 anchors it.

### Reasoning

First, the tariff map sets the size of the gap, not the badge: the two widest spreads sit inside the EU ($9,293 and $9,216), where two producer-specific countervailing duties compound on top of 10% duty and 19–20% VAT, and the narrowest is Thailand at $6,078 in a zero-duty, 7% VAT lane. Second, the percentage premiums run opposite to intuition — the cheaper car carries the heavier premium in all seven markets (+93.7% against +85.6% in Germany) because a fixed block of $6,600 in that market weighs more on a $16,260 base than on a $21,940 one. Read dollars for decisions; percentages describe burden. Third, most of the gap is not duty: $5,680 of the $9,216 German spread is the base-price difference, with $3,536 of compounded tax and duty on top — an importer negotiating harder at the factory than at the port is fighting the smaller half of the problem. Fourth, nothing in these tables covers warranty provisioning, parts pipeline or resale support, and those are the numbers that decide whether year three is profitable for either car.
