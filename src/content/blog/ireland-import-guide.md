## TL;DR

A Chinese EV imported into Ireland pays the EU's standard 10% import duty, then a brand-specific countervailing duty — BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC/MG 35.3%, most other producers 20.7% — and then 23% VAT on the whole stack: a BYD Seal lands at about **$45,384 (+83.8%)**, roughly **$6,544 more than the same car in the UK**, where no countervailing duty applies. Ireland then charges its own registration tax, VRT, on a different base entirely — and the relief that currently zeroes VRT for most EVs runs only to **31 December 2026**.

## Key numbers

- **Import duty:** 10% of the CIF value — the EU's most-favoured-nation rate for battery-electric passenger cars (CN 8703 80 10), collected at import
- **Countervailing duty (CVD):** EU-wide and brand-specific — BYD 17.0%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC (MG, Maxus) 35.3%, other cooperating producers 20.7%, non-cooperating producers 35.3%, Tesla Shanghai 7.8%; a five-year measure adopted in October 2024, and it bites battery-electric cars only (PHEVs and EREVs pay none)
- **VAT:** 23% — charged on the duty-inclusive value, so it compounds instead of adding
- **VRT (registration tax):** CO2-banded at 7%–41% of the open market selling price; a battery-electric car sits in the lowest band, and a relief of up to €5,000 currently covers the charge for eligible EVs registered before 31 December 2026 — full relief up to €40,000 OMSP, reduced relief between €40,000 and €50,000, none above €50,000
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal, $24,690 ex-factory → **$45,384.52 landed (+83.8%)**, before VRT
- **Ireland vs UK (same Seal):** $45,384.52 vs $38,840.80 — a **$6,543.72** gap created almost entirely by the countervailing duty and its VAT cascade
- **Ireland vs Germany (same Seal):** $45,384.52 vs $44,413.48 — Dublin is $971 above Berlin, because both pay the same duty lines and only the VAT rate and fee block differ
- **RHD:** Ireland is one of only three EU member states that drive on the left (Malta and Cyprus are the others) — and by far the largest of the three
- **Registration clock:** imported vehicles must be registered within 30 days of entry; the National Car Testing Service (NCTS) registers on Revenue's behalf

## The terms that decide this market

- **Landed cost** — what a car costs to get from a Chinese factory to an import-cleared, registrable state in the destination country: ex-factory price, duty, countervailing duty, VAT, freight, clearance, compliance, registration and inland transport. The figures in this article are EV Hub model estimates, not quotes.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance and freight; the customs value the 10% duty is calculated on.
- **Countervailing duty (CVD)** — the EU's anti-subsidy tariff on China-built battery-electric cars, set per producer group rather than per model. It stacks on top of the standard duty before VAT touches anything, and Ireland applies it as part of the EU customs union. This is the single biggest difference between Ireland and its nearest neighbour.
- **VAT** — value-added tax. Ireland's standard rate is 23%, charged at import on the duty-inclusive value. For VAT-registered businesses it is normally deductible; for private buyers it is simply cost.
- **VRT (Vehicle Registration Tax)** — Ireland's own registration tax, charged once when the car is entered on the Irish register, calculated on the **OMSP** and banded by WLTP CO2 emissions. It is not part of the customs stack and is not included in EV Hub's landing figures in this article — it comes on top, with its own relief rules.
- **OMSP** — the open market selling price: the Revenue-assessed market value of the car in Ireland, which Revenue notes is "usually slightly different" from the invoice value. If a model is not in the VRT calculator, Revenue determines its OMSP using market indicators including UK and Irish trade guides, advertisements and experts.
- **RHD** — right-hand drive. Ireland drives on the left and new cars sold there are RHD; China's domestic lineup is left-hand drive, so the models that can be distributed at scale are the RHD export builds.
- **NCTS** — the National Car Testing Service, which registers vehicles on Revenue's behalf and collects VRT at the point of registration.

## What a Chinese EV pays at the Irish border

| Layer | Rate (China-origin BEV) | Levied on | In EV Hub's landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **10%** | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **17%–35.3%**, brand-specific | CIF value plus duty, in our model | Yes |
| VAT | **23%** | duty-inclusive value | Yes |
| VRT | **7%** minimum band for a BEV | OMSP at registration | **No — excluded** |
| Registration fee block | ~$200 recorded for Ireland | — | Yes (the fee only, not the VRT) |
| NOx levy | separate from the CO2 charge; a BEV has no tailpipe NOx | — | **No — excluded** |

The stack tracks the EU mainland almost to the euro, and that is the point: what looks like an Irish question is really an EU customs-union question. Two cars leave the same Chinese port for Dublin and Dover. Both are right-hand drive, both share the same export spec, both pay 10% on arrival. Then the UK lets its car through with VAT and nothing else, while Ireland adds the brand's countervailing duty — and taxes it again inside the VAT base. Across the models in this guide that single missing line is worth $2,401 to $10,970 per car.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal lands in Ireland

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $2,469.00 |
| Countervailing duty (17% of CIF + duty, BYD) | $4,617.03 |
| VAT (23% of the duty-inclusive value) | $7,308.49 |
| RoRo freight | $2,000.00 |
| Customs clearance | $350.00 |
| Certification (WVTA / CoC pathway) | $3,250.00 |
| Registration | $200.00 |
| Inland transport | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$45,384.52 (+83.8%)** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs` — here `24,690 × 1.10 × 1.17 × 1.23 + 6,300`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 15 September 2026. For how the model is built, see [Landed cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

Treat $45,384 as a floor, not a showroom estimate. It excludes VRT (which we get to below), any dealer margin, and any currency moves between order and arrival.

Two patterns sit inside this table, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is scale: a fixed block of about $6,300 — freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland transport — does not shrink for cheap cars. It is why an $8,320 Neta V lands 139% above its ex-factory price while a $38,000 Zeekr 001 lands 77.3% above, and why across the whole 517-model catalogue Irish premiums run from +243.3% (a $3,500 microcar) to +38.5% (a $197,180 limousine). [The sticker price is never the landed price](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/), and the cheaper the car, the more violently that is true.

The second pattern is the brand's countervailing duty rate. The MG 4 and the BYD Seagull leave China within $750 of each other, but the MG lands $3,586 further up the board — mostly because SAIC pays the EU's top rate of 35.3% while BYD pays 17%. The rate, not the price tag, ranks these cars. The full brand breakdowns are in our [BYD 17% CVD math](/blog/byd-countervailing-duty-landed-cost/), [SAIC 35.3% explainer](/blog/saic-35-percent-countervailing-duty/) and [Geely 18.8% guide](/blog/geely-188-countervailing-duty/).

## Ireland vs the UK and Germany: what the countervailing duty really costs

| Model | Base | Ireland | UK | Germany | Ireland − UK |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BYD Seagull | $8,940 | $20,452 | $18,050 | $20,291 | +$2,401 |
| MG 4 | $9,690 | $24,038 | $19,040 | $23,761 | +$4,998 |
| BYD Seal | $24,690 | $45,384 | $38,840 | $44,413 | +$6,544 |
| Zeekr 001 | $38,000 | $67,379 | $56,410 | $65,693 | +$10,970 |

Against the UK, the gap is the countervailing duty plus the VAT charged on it — and it scales with both the brand's rate and the price of the car. The 17% Seagull gap is $2,401; the 18.8% Zeekr 001 gap is $10,970 — more than four times larger, on a car about three times the landed price. Distribution plans that treat "UK & Ireland" as one region run on the same RHD supply chain and the same shipping lanes, but they need two different tax models.

Against the EU mainland, the gap nearly closes, and the leftover is instructive. The same Seal costs $971 more in Dublin than in Berlin — not because any duty line differs, but because Ireland's 23% VAT rate sits four points above Germany's 19%, and it compounds on the duty-inclusive value. Ireland's fixed block is actually $300 cheaper ($6,300 versus $6,600) — the net $971 gap is pure VAT arithmetic on the duty-inclusive value. On our numbers Dublin sits between Stockholm ($45,890 for the same Seal) and Berlin, well below Denmark's $49,320 — this is not the EU's most expensive landing, just one of its more quietly expensive ones. The within-EU spreads are quantified in [Germany vs Netherlands vs France](/blog/germany-netherlands-france-import-comparison/).

One wrinkle matters for product planning: the countervailing duty is BEV-only in our model. A BYD Tang PHEV carries no CVD into either market, so the Irish–UK gap collapses to $883 ($40,463 versus $39,580). Plug-ins pay no anti-subsidy tariff — but they also fail the VRT relief test below, because the relief is reserved for cars "powered only by an electric motor". The exemption that saves the duty costs them the registration relief.

## The VRT layer: CO2 bands, a €5,000 relief, and a December 2026 deadline

Everything above is the customs stack. Ireland then charges Vehicle Registration Tax at the point of registration — a different tax, on a different base, with a different clock.

VRT for category A vehicles (passenger cars) is banded by WLTP CO2 emissions, at rates set since 1 January 2022: from 7% (0–50 g/km, minimum €140) through to 41% (over 190 g/km, minimum €820) of the OMSP, plus a separate NOx levy. A battery-electric car registers zero tailpipe NOx and sits in the lowest 7% band — and then the relief does the heavy lifting.

Under the current rules, series-production passenger cars (and category B commercial vehicles) powered only by an electric motor and registered before 31 December 2026 are eligible for a VRT reduction of up to €5,000:

| OMSP of the car | Relief available | Illustration (Revenue's own examples and bands) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to €40,000 | Up to €5,000 | €40,000 OMSP → 7% = €2,800 → relief €2,800 → **€0 payable**; €30,000 OMSP → €2,100 → **€0 payable** |
| €40,000–€50,000 | Reduced relief | The reduction scale is not published on the guidance we reviewed — confirm your figure (UNKNOWN) |
| Above €50,000 | None | €60,000 OMSP → 7% ≈ €4,200 → **€4,200 payable** (worked from Revenue's published band) |

The relief is applied automatically at registration in an NCTS centre or through the Revenue Online Service — no forms, no application. For most Chinese EVs in Irish showrooms, whose OMSPs sit well under €40,000, VRT therefore nets to zero today.

Two things break that comfort. First, the ceiling: a flagship import — Zeekr 009, NIO ET9, Xiaomi SU7 Ultra territory — pays the full 7% regardless of today's relief, because the relief disappears entirely above €50,000 OMSP. Second, and bigger, the deadline: the relief is legislated only for registrations before 31 December 2026. If it lapses — and whether Budget 2027 extends, scales or removes it is unknown at the time of writing — every BEV import owes the full band again: roughly €2,100 on a €30,000 car, €2,800 on a €40,000 car, by the same published percentages. On the models in this guide, that is a swing of a few percentage points of total landing cost, concentrated exactly where volume sits.

Three further notes for importers. VRT is assessed on the OMSP, so how Revenue values your specific make and trim matters as much as the rate — for makes not commonly distributed in Ireland there is a VRT Estimate Form to get a read before purchase. Category B vans have their own CO2 table since 1 July 2025 (8% up to 120 g/km, 13.3% above, minimums €160/€266), and certain electric vans fall under a flat €200 charge — worth checking if your import is a commercial vehicle rather than a car. And the €200 registration line in our landed-cost model is the administrative fee block, not VRT: the two figures are separate and both apply.

## Right-hand drive: the EU's largest left-driving market

Ireland is the EU's RHD exception — the largest of the bloc's three left-driving members by a wide margin, with Malta and Cyprus the only other two. That makes Ireland structurally different from every other market in this series except the UK: it is served by right-hand-drive export builds from China, a subset of each brand's lineup, and the same build logic that decides which models can be sold in Britain decides what can be sold in Ireland. Our [LHD vs RHD market map](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) covers which models travel and which do not.

The supply-side consequence is easy to underestimate. A brand that ships LHD-only cars into Germany cannot simply redirect a container to Dublin; the RHD requirement filters the catalog before the tariff discussion starts. The good news is that most large Chinese brands selling in Ireland already run RHD export programs. The catch is that the RHD subset rarely covers the entire range.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **Confirm the producer's countervailing duty rate for your exact model** — it follows the producer group, not the badge: SAIC's rate applies across MG and Maxus; Geely's across Zeekr and Lynk & Co; BYD's to its sub-brands.
2. **Register within 30 days of arrival.** The customs declaration proving payment of duty and VAT is required by NCTS at registration.
3. **Price the VRT before you buy, not after.** Check the model on the VRT calculator; for rare makes, use the VRT Estimate Form. Assume full 7% on anything above €50,000 OMSP.
4. **Watch the calendar if you want the relief.** The €5,000 EV reduction applies to registrations before 31 December 2026. Everything after that is a policy question, not a math question.
5. **Pick the right homologation route.** An existing EU whole-vehicle type approval with a per-VIN Certificate of Conformity is the fast lane; single-vehicle approval is slower and costlier. See [WVTA vs single-vehicle approval](/blog/wvta-vs-single-vehicle-approval/).
6. **Check the charging inlet.** EU-spec cars use Type 2/CCS2; China-market cars use GB/T. For fleet use, import the EU-spec build — [the adapter path has a cost of its own](/blog/ccs2-vs-gbt-charging/).
7. **For businesses, plan the VAT cash flow.** Import VAT is normally deductible for VAT-registered traders, but it is paid at the border first.
8. **Buyer-side incentives are a separate question.** SEAI grant terms for EV buyers were not verified in this research pass (UNKNOWN) — check the current position directly before you budget around them.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, the Irish import case is not really about the tariff — the countervailing duty and the 23% VAT largely shadow the EU mainland — it is about the December 2026 deadline: the VRT relief that currently zeroes the registration bill for most EVs runs out at the end of this year, and nothing has been legislated to replace it.

**Reasoning.** First, the UK comparison is the one that changes decisions. Ireland and the UK pull from the same RHD factories and ship on the same routes, but the countervailing duty Ireland pays and the UK does not is worth $2,401 to $10,970 per car in our records — enough that "UK & Ireland" is not one market for pricing purposes, and pretending otherwise overpays or underprices one side by thousands. Second, within the EU Ireland's position is premium but not extreme: roughly $971 above Germany on a Seal, held there by the 23% VAT rate compounding over the same duty lines every other member state charges. Third, the deadline is the real swing factor. A relief that is worth about €2,100–€2,800 per car at typical OMSPs, and more on €40,000-plus cars, currently has no life beyond 31 December 2026; anyone planning a 2027 import against today's landing costs is implicitly betting on a Budget decision that has not been made.

**Disclosure.** This is my analysis of published Revenue guidance, the EU regulation behind the duty rates, and EV Hub's cost records — opinion, not legal, tax or customs advice. Whether the VRT relief survives December 2026, the exact OMSP Revenue will assign to any specific car, and the reduced relief scale between €40,000 and €50,000 OMSP are all open questions flagged above; confirm them with Revenue and a customs broker before committing capital.
