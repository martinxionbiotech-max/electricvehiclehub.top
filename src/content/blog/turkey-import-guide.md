## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to Turkey costs a **40% import duty plus 20% VAT** on the modeled number — and that is before the country's own special consumption tax (ÖTV), which runs **25–75%** for battery-electric cars. A BYD Seal priced at $24,690 ex-factory China lands at roughly **$45,729 in Turkey (+85.2%)**, which is *above* the same car's German landed cost despite the EU's countervailing duty. The threshold is not really the tariff; it is whether your model's power and price band land you in the 25% ÖTV bracket or the 75% one.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty:** 40% effective on the customs value (10% MFN duty plus a 30% additional duty on non-FTA vehicle imports; the September 2025 decree replaced the older China-specific surcharges)
- **Minimum levy:** the additional duty for electric cars is **30% or USD 8,500 per vehicle, whichever is higher** — on cheap models the floor is the binding number
- **VAT (KDV):** 20%, applied on the duty-paid base
- **ÖTV:** 25–75% depending on motor output and tax-base bracket; the lowest EV band rose to 25% after the July 2025 schedule revision
- **Countervailing duty:** none — Turkey has no EU-style anti-subsidy tariff
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal ($24,690 base) → **$45,729 landed in Turkey (+85.2%)**
- **The real hurdle:** ÖTV band placement plus the USD 8,500 minimum levy, not the duty line

## How the Turkish tax stack actually works

Turkey's vehicle tax is three layers deep, and they compound in a specific order:

1. **Customs duty at 40%.** The 10% MFN rate applies, then an additional duty of 30% (or USD 8,500, whichever is greater) applies to motor cars from countries without a free-trade agreement with Türkiye. EY's summary of Decree no. 10486 confirms the structure: the previous China-specific surcharges were withdrawn and replaced by blanket additional liabilities based on powertrain — 30% or USD 8,500 for electric cars.
2. **ÖTV on the duty-paid value.** The Special Consumption Tax is the layer that decides outcomes. After the July 2025 revision, EV rates sit in a 25–75% band, keyed to motor power and price bracket rather than to emissions.
3. **20% VAT on the cumulative base.** VAT applies after duty and, in the real customs calculation, after ÖTV — which is exactly why ÖTV shapes the final price so violently.

The USD 8,500 floor matters more than people expect. On a Wuling Hongguang MINI EV with a $5,000 declared value, a percentage duty would be trivial. The floor turns it into a real cost. That is why Turkey's cheap-EV segment effectively does not exist for imports.

The pattern is inverted from what most buyers assume: the *cheaper* the car, the worse the percentage. A MINI EV more than doubles in price. A Zeekr 001 rises 79%. Absolute tax is what scales, so small cars absorb a disproportionate share.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in Turkey

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (40% of base) | $9,876 |
| VAT (20% of base + duty) | $6,913 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $4,250 |
| **Total landed** | **$45,729** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 2026-09-15.

**Where this model stops:** ÖTV is **not** in that $45,729. EV Hub's tariff model captures the statutory duty and the consumption-tax-equivalent VAT/GST layer, but not Türkiye's ÖTV. If ÖTV applies at 25% on the duty-paid value of $34,566, that adds about $8,642 before VAT; at the 75% band it adds about $25,924. Add VAT on top and the real showroom number is far above $45,729. Treat the table as a floor, not a forecast.

## The real threshold

Three things gate a Turkish import, in order of severity:

1. **ÖTV band placement.** Power output and the tax-base bracket decide whether your car carries 25% or 75%. An importer with a 160 kW crossover and an importer with an 80 kW hatchback are not in the same business.
2. **The USD 8,500 minimum.** It kills the sub-$15,000 segment outright.
3. **Documentation and importer eligibility.** Turkey requires proper customs documentation and a legitimate importing entity. The specific licensing path for a first-time private importer is something we could not verify from primary sources — **UNKNOWN**.

## What buyers should check before committing

- **Which ÖTV bracket does this exact model land in?** Ask for the band, not the model name — the band is the price.
- **Does the USD 8,500 floor bind on this vehicle?** On anything under roughly $28,000 customs value, check both calculations.
- **Is there an EU-routed alternative?** Vehicles from EU member states or FTA partners avoid the additional duty entirely. That is a legitimate sourcing strategy, not a loophole.
- **What is the buyer's price ceiling?** Turkey's own EV buyers are highly rate-sensitive. A model that clears customs at 86% total tax burden may clear customs and never clear a showroom.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** In my view, Turkey is not a high-tariff market with a tax problem — it is a tax market with a tariff problem, and no importer should quote Turkey's 40% duty as the headline number.

**Reasoning:** First, the 40% duty is only about half the story; the 25–75% ÖTV and 20% VAT sit on top of it, and Ember's analysis puts the total burden on a lowest-band EV near 50% and up to 86% once the car crosses into a higher bracket. Second, the USD 8,500 floor is doing more policy work than the percentage — it deliberately excludes the segment where Chinese EVs are strongest, which tells you the intent is protecting domestic assembly, not raising revenue. Third, the gap between the published tariff and the actual showroom price is where most import feasibility studies quietly fail, because the models that look viable at 40% are not viable at 40% plus ÖTV.

**Disclosure:** This is my interpretation of published tax law and tariff data, not legal or tax advice. ÖTV brackets and minimum levies change by presidential decree; verify the current schedule with a Turkish customs broker before pricing anything.
