## TL;DR

A [BYD Atto 3](/vehicles/byd-atto-3/) and a Tesla Model Y are different sizes of car chasing the same shortlist — a compact import against a mid-size retail product — and on landed cost, the import wins by a distance: **$31,503 in Germany, $27,713 in the UK and $23,736 in Australia**, against a Model Y that starts at **€38,970 (~$44,710), £41,990 (~$56,136) and A$58,900 before on-road costs (~$41,878)** in the same three markets. The gap runs **$13,208 to $28,423** — real money, attached to a real segment gap.

## Key statistics

- **Atto 3 base price:** $16,260 China ex-factory MSRP — 49.92 kWh Blade battery, 430 km CLTC, 150 kW, 4,455 mm long
- **Model Y reference prices:** from €38,970 (Germany; list €39,990) · £41,990 (UK) · A$58,900 before on-road costs (Australia) — one size class up at 4,792 mm
- **Atto 3 landed:** Germany $31,503 (+93.7%) · UK $27,713 (+70.4%) · Australia $23,736 (+46.0%)
- **Model Y converted to USD** at 17 Sep 2026 rates (EUR/USD 1.1473 · GBP/USD 1.3369 · AUD/USD 0.7110): ≈$44,710 · ≈$56,136 · ≈$41,878
- **The gap:** $13,208 (Germany) · $28,423 (UK) · $18,142 (Australia)
- **Why it moves:** BYD pays the EU's 17% countervailing duty plus 19% VAT in Germany; the UK charges 10% duty with no CVD; Australia charges no duty at all
- **The caveat that decides deals:** the Atto 3 number carries no importer margin; the Model Y number already carries Tesla's

## First, the segment check nobody runs

Most "Atto 3 vs Model Y" tables put two cars side by side that are not in the same class. Measure them before you price them:

| Specification | BYD Atto 3 | Tesla Model Y |
|---|---|---|
| Length × width × height | 4,455 × 1,875 × 1,615 mm | 4,792 × 1,921 × 1,624 mm |
| Wheelbase | 2,720 mm | 2,890 mm |
| Curb weight | 1,625 kg | 1,921 kg |
| Battery | 49.92 kWh Blade | — (not published for the Standard in our sources) |
| Range, as quoted | 430 km CLTC | 314 miles ≈ 505 km WLTP (Standard, UK) |
| Motor | 150 kW / 310 Nm | — (not verified in our sources) |

Where a Model Y figure could not be verified against a listed source, the cell reads "—". That is deliberate: filling those gaps with guesses is how bad comparisons get built. What is not in doubt is the footprint — **337 mm of length and 170 mm of wheelbase** separate a B-segment crossover from a D-segment family SUV.

One range warning before the table gets quoted out of context: 430 km CLTC is not 430 real kilometres, and it is not comparable to a WLTP number. Our [CLTC vs WLTP explainer](/blog/cltc-vs-wltp-range/) covers why; the short version is that CLTC flatters and the two cycles should never be subtracted from each other.

## The landed-cost matrix

Atto 3 figures are EV Hub landed-cost estimates (ex-factory base, duty, CVD where applicable, VAT/GST, freight and fixed costs). Model Y figures are local retail prices from the sources below, converted at 17 September 2026 rates.

| Market | Atto 3 landed (model) | Model Y local price | Model Y converted | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | $31,503 (+93.7%) | from €38,970 | ≈ $44,710 | $13,208 |
| United Kingdom | $27,713 (+70.4%) | £41,990 | ≈ $56,136 | $28,423 |
| Australia | $23,736 (+46.0%) | A$58,900* | ≈ $41,878 | $18,142 |

*Sticker before on-road costs. CarExpert's price table displays drive-away pricing on a Sydney basis from about A$63,963 (≈ $45,478), which widens the Australian gap to $21,742. Either basis, same direction.

Now the honest framing, because this table is where most comparisons go wrong. The Atto 3 column is a **landed import cost** — it contains no importer margin, no warranty provisioning and no dealer spread. The Model Y column is a **retail price** — it already contains Tesla's duty bill, logistics, VAT, support network and margin. One column is a cost; the other is a finished product. Comparing them is legitimate for an importer asking "what headroom do I have against this competing shelf price?" It is not legitimate as a like-for-like verdict on the cars; see [why the sticker price is never the landed price](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/).

## Worked example: how $16,260 becomes $31,503 in Germany

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (China ex-factory MSRP) | $16,260.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $1,626.00 |
| Countervailing duty (BYD, 17%) | $3,040.62 |
| VAT (19%) | $3,976.06 |
| RoRo freight | $2,000.00 |
| Customs clearance | $350.00 |
| Certification / homologation | $3,250.00 |
| Registration | $500.00 |
| Inland transport | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$31,502.68** |

Formula: `16,260 × 1.10 × 1.17 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 31,502.68`. The order matters — standard duty first, countervailing duty on the duty-paid value, VAT on everything above it, then the flat block. Read the full methodology in [our docs](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

## Why the premium nearly halves from market to market

Same car, three tariff worlds:

- **Germany (+93.7%):** 10% duty, plus BYD's 17% countervailing duty, plus 19% VAT — a stack that roughly doubles the car before logistics.
- **United Kingdom (+70.4%):** 10% duty and 20% VAT, **no countervailing duty**. The car lands at $27,713.
- **Australia (+46.0%):** zero duty under the China FTA lane and 10% GST. The car lands at $23,736 — about $7,767 cheaper to land than Germany — the same car, a different jurisdiction. 

The gap against the Model Y moves with this map. It is widest in the UK ($28,423), where Tesla's local price carries the strongest sticker relative to the import's landed cost, and narrowest in Germany ($13,208), where both numbers are squeezed by the same high-tax environment — the import by duty, the retail price by VAT.

## Where this comparison can go wrong

1. **FOB versus OTR.** The single biggest error in buyer math is subtracting an ex-factory number from a retail number. The two are not the same unit of anything. This piece keeps them clearly labelled; most tables do not.
2. **Mixing range cycles.** CLTC and WLTP figures are not interchangeable, on these cars or any others.
3. **Ignoring the margin you must add.** The $31,503 does not sell the car; it buys the car. Every dollar of importer and dealer margin narrows the gap against the Model Y.
4. **Forgetting the service question.** A Tesla comes with a network. An imported Atto 3 comes with whatever your importer builds. Warranty path, parts pipeline and resale — none of them appear in the landed table, and all of them decide whether year three is profitable.
5. **Treating today's duty rates as furniture.** The EU's countervailing-duty regime is under periodic review and legal challenge; non-EU rates can move too. Model the deal at current rates and stress it at plus or minus a few points.

## What compact SUV buyers should actually do

If the car is for your own driveway and you value a national service network, the Model Y's price buys things the import math cannot show you — and the segment gap above is real. If you are importing to sell, or for a fleet where you control service yourself, the Atto 3's landed position is strong in every market we ran; run your actual margin, warranty reserve and resale assumption through the [landed-cost calculator](/landed-cost-calculator/) before you commit, because those three numbers, not the sticker, decide whether the "$13k saving" survives contact with year one.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, the Atto 3's $13,208–$28,423 landed advantage over a Tesla Model Y is real arithmetic attached to the wrong headline: buyers are not looking at a cheaper Model Y, they are looking at a smaller car and an unbuilt relationship — a compact crossover whose warranty path, parts pipeline and resale curve the importer still has to construct. Treat it as a segment decision, not a bargain.

**Reasoning.** First, the physical gap is plain once measured — 337 mm shorter, 170 mm less wheelbase, 296 kg lighter — and a smaller car costing less is not arbitrage; it is the market working as designed. Second, the tariff map, not the car, decides how big the gap gets: the same Atto 3 lands at $31,503 in Germany with BYD's 17% countervailing duty attached, and $23,736 in Australia where no duty applies at all — nearly $7,800 of the difference is jurisdiction, not product. Third, the number absent from every one of these tables is the one that decides the deal in year three: who warranties the car, and who buys it back. An importer who cannot answer both questions is quietly discounting someone else's risk into their own price.
