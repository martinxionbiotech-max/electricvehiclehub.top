## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to Italy means paying the full EU tariff stack — **10% standard duty, a brand-specific countervailing duty (BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC/MG 35.3%, most other exporters 20.7%), then 22% VAT on the duty-paid value** — which lands a BYD Seal at about **$45,117 (+82.7%)** in EV Hub's records, the most expensive of the EU's four largest car markets for that car. Italy's twist is that it is Stellantis's home market: the cheapest Chinese EV selling in volume there, the Leapmotor T03, is distributed by Stellantis itself, and BYD has been in talks to take over underused Italian plants — so the "import" story and the domestic industry story keep crossing wires.

## Key numbers

- **Import duty:** 10% MFN (most-favoured-nation) on battery-electric passenger cars, applied at every EU entry point
- **Countervailing duty (CVD):** brand-specific and BEV-only — BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC (MG) 35.3%, Tesla's China-built cars 7.8%, other cooperating exporters 20.7%, non-cooperating 35.3%; PHEVs and EREVs currently pay 10% and nothing else
- **VAT:** 22% — the highest of the EU's four largest car markets (Germany 19%, France 20%, Spain 21%) and one of the highest in the bloc
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 ex-factory → **$45,117 landed (+82.7%)**
- **Cheapest Chinese EV in our Italy table:** Leapmotor T03 at **$20,021 (+137.2%)** — and the best-selling BEV in Italy in the first seven months of 2026 (22,353 registrations, roughly a quarter of the country's BEV market)
- **Italy vs Germany on the same Seal:** $45,117 vs $44,413 — a spread of $703, almost all of it the 3-point VAT difference
- **The 2026 wrinkles:** a refinanced ecobonus worth up to €13,750, a pending Brussels decision on extending CVDs to plug-in hybrids, and BYD–Stellantis talks over factories in Italy

## The terms that decide this market

- **Landed cost** — what the car costs by the time it is cleared and registered: the China price, plus duty, plus CVD, plus VAT, plus freight, clearance, certification, registration and inland transport. It is not a showroom price, and it is not a quote.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance and freight; the customs-value concept the duty stack is assessed on. Our model starts from the ex-factory price and adds freight as a separate line.
- **MFN duty** — the EU's common external tariff for cars, 10%, identical in Rome, Hamburg, Rotterdam or Athens because the EU is a single customs territory.
- **CVD (countervailing duty)** — the anti-subsidy tariff Brussels imposed on China-built BEVs in October 2024 after its anti-subsidy investigation. It is set by producer, not by destination — a BYD pays 17% at every EU border, an MG pays 35.3%.
- **VAT** — Italy's standard rate is 22%, charged on the duty- and CVD-paid value at import/registration. There is no EV-specific VAT reduction in Italy.
- **Ecobonus** — Italy's national purchase-incentive scheme for low-emission vehicles. It was relaunched in October 2025 and refinanced for 2026; for passenger EVs the support runs from €6,000 to €11,000, rising to €13,750 for lower-income households scrapping an old car.
- **WVTA** — Whole Vehicle Type Approval, the EU-wide homologation route; cars not built to it need national or individual approval instead, which is where a private import's compliance budget can blow up.

## What a Chinese EV actually pays at the Italian border

| Layer | Rate for a China-origin BEV | Levied on | In EV Hub's landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **10%** (EU MFN) | customs value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **17% / 18.8% / 35.3% / 20.7%** by brand | duty-paid value | Yes |
| VAT | **22%** | duty- and CVD-paid value | Yes |
| Registration (IPT) | ~$250 in our records; often waived for EVs at provincial level | — | Yes |
| Freight, clearance, certification, inland | ~$6,100 fixed | — | Yes |

That is the whole story of why Italy is not a cheap landing. Most markets we track have one or two double-digit layers; Italy stacks three, and the third one — 22% — is the highest among the EU's big four. The compensation, to the extent there is one, sits below the customs line: an EV in Italy pays no CO₂-based registration tax, many provinces waive the registration fee outright, and the annual circulation tax (bollo) is fully exempt for five years from first registration, then charged at 25% of the standard rate. For a private buyer or a fleet, those are real euros — but they arrive after the border bill, not before it.

Three patterns are worth naming, because they explain most of the table.

First, the percentage premium falls as the price rises — the fixed import block of roughly $6,350 hits a $5,000 microcar proportionally far harder (+208.6%) than a $38,000 Zeekr (+76.1%). This is the same arithmetic every market in our dataset shows, and it is why the cheapest Chinese cars are rarely the cheapest *imports*.

Second, the brand tax is visible in the middle of the table: the MG 4 pays 35.3% CVD and lands at $23,944, while the BYD Atto 2 pays 17% and lands at $22,899 despite a base price $850 higher. SAIC's tariff rate is the single biggest variable in this market for any given size of car — we covered why in our [SAIC 35.3% CVD explainer](/blog/saic-35-percent-countervailing-duty/) and the [MG budget-EV paradox piece](/blog/mg-budget-ev-tariff-paradox/).

Third, the PHEV row sits in the middle of a BEV sandwich. The BYD Seal 06 DM-i carries no CVD at all — plug-in hybrids were excluded from the anti-subsidy measures — so its landing premium (+79.6%) is the second-lowest in the table. We will come back to that, because Brussels has been preparing to close exactly this gap.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in Italy

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (10%) | $2,469 |
| Countervailing duty (17%, on duty-paid value) | $4,617 |
| VAT (22%, on duty- and CVD-paid value) | $6,991 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $6,350 |
| **Total landed** | **$45,117** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and vehicle records dated 15 September 2026. The compounding is the part most price comparisons miss: the CVD is charged on the duty-paid value, and VAT is charged on both. Ten percent plus seventeen plus twenty-two is not forty-nine — the layers compound to roughly a 57% uplift on the base price before the fixed block, which is why the Seal lands at +82.7% all-in. For how the model is built, see [How our landed-cost model works](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

For contrast, run the same math for an MG: the duty stays 10%, but the SAIC CVD of 35.3% pushes a $9,690 MG 4 to $23,944 — a +147.1% premium. Hold the car constant and change only the rate, and the same base lands at about $21,565 with BYD's 17% CVD (+122.5%): roughly 25 points of premium and about $2,380 of cost, purely from the brand-level rate.

## The same car across the EU's big four

| Market | VAT | BYD Seal landed | Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 19% | $44,413 | +79.9% |
| France | 20% | $44,631 | +80.8% |
| Spain | 21% | $44,659 | +80.9% |
| Italy | 22% | $45,117 | +82.7% |

Within the EU, the duty and CVD are identical for this car — the only variables are VAT and each country's registration fee. On the Seal, Italy's 22% rate adds $953 of VAT versus Germany, partly offset by a lower registration line in our records ($250 vs $500), for a net gap of $703. The honest interpretation: Italy is the priciest of the big four by about 1.6% of landed cost. That is not a reason to route a car into Hamburg and sell it in Rome — the car is registered where it lives, and a vehicle's final registration country determines the VAT owed; our [Germany vs Netherlands vs France comparison](/blog/germany-netherlands-france-import-comparison/) walks through why entry-country arbitrage does not work in practice.

The more useful cross-border context sits just outside the customs union. The same Seal lands at $38,840 in the UK (+57.3%) and $33,807 in Switzerland (+36.9%), because neither levies the EU CVD. Swap 20.7% for 35.3% on a brand like MG and that gap widens further — the EU duty wall, not the Italian one, is what makes Italy expensive.

Italy's position on that wall has been consistent from the start: then-foreign minister Antonio Tajani said in September 2024 that Rome supported the Commission's proposed duties "to protect the competitiveness of our companies", and when member states voted on 4 October 2024, Italy was among the ten in favour (Germany, Hungary, Malta, Slovakia and Slovenia voted against; twelve states abstained). Industrial policy has been two-track ever since — back the tariff, court the factories: Rome has held talks with Chinese carmakers including Dongfeng and Chery about building in Italy.

## The Stellantis factor: a home market under pressure

Italy is where Stellantis is rooted — Fiat, Lancia, Alfa Romeo, Maserati, most of the group's historic plants. It is also where the group has had its hardest years. Stellantis' Italian vehicle production fell 20% in 2025 to 379,706 units, with passenger cars down 24.5% to 213,706 — roughly half the 751,000 units of 2023 and the lowest volume since 1954. Italy dropped out of the world's top-20 car-producing countries. Mirafiori, the historic Turin site, was the lone bright spot (+16% to about 30,000 units, helped by the late Fiat 500 hybrid) and remains far below its stated 200,000-unit capacity; Cassino recorded its worst year ever (just under 19,000 units); Melfi fell 47%. For scale: Stellantis' Vigo plant in Spain built nearly 600,000 vehicles in 2025, almost twice the combined output of all Italian sites.

Two things followed from that — and both touch China directly.

The first is a division of labour inside Italy. In June 2026 Stellantis told unions it will not close any Italian plants, assigning each a specific role under its new business plan: Pomigliano focuses on affordable electric vehicles, Mirafiori on small cars and industrial innovation, Melfi, Cassino and Modena on premium and luxury offerings, Atessa on light commercial vehicles. "Affordable EVs" is the operative phrase — the segment where Chinese imports have been strongest.

The second is that Chinese capital and Chinese brands are being invited into the gap. BYD said in May 2026 it was negotiating with Stellantis and other carmakers about taking over underused European factories, with executive vice president Stella Li naming countries "such as Italy" and adding that BYD would prefer to run any plant on its own rather than through a joint venture. Stellantis' own China entanglement runs through Leapmotor: Stellantis has been Leapmotor's largest shareholder since 2023 (about 21%), and their Leapmotor International joint venture (Stellantis 51%) sells and will build Leapmotor cars outside China — T03 and C10 since 2024, a B10 line at Zaragoza in Spain potentially from 2026, an Opel EV on a shared line from 2028, and a planned transfer of the Villaverde plant in Madrid to Leapmotor's Spanish subsidiary. The JV already runs more than 850 sales and service points in Europe and delivered over 40,000 vehicles there in 2025.

Now connect that to the actual Italian market data: BYD registered 33,973 passenger cars in Italy in January–July 2026, up nearly 195% year-on-year, lifting its share to 3.2% from 1.18%; in July alone it registered 4,466 cars (+127%). Italy's total BEV registrations for the period were 86,003, up 70.4%. And of those, 22,353 — about 26% — were Leapmotor T03s: a Chinese-developed city car sold through Stellantis' distribution arm, and the model that Il Sole 24 ORE singled out when it reported that Italy's eco-bonus "mainly favoured models produced by Chinese manufacturers, particularly Leapmotor". BYD, for its part, says more than one in four plug-in hybrids sold in Italy is a BYD, and that its compact Dolphin Surf was the country's best-selling BEV in July. Italy is one of Europe's top-five car markets, and new entrants keep arriving: Xpeng formally entered the country in May 2025, citing the market's size and a charging network expanding about 35% a year. It is also, increasingly, a test case for the contradictions of the EU's China strategy.

## The incentive layer: an ecobonus that keeps moving

The single biggest non-tariff number in the Italian market is the ecobonus. Per the European Commission's Alternative Fuels Observatory, the scheme was refinanced for 2026: contributions for passenger EVs range from €6,000 (no scrappage) to €11,000 (with scrappage), rising 25% for households with an ISEE below €30,000 — up to €13,750 when scrapping a Euro 0–2 car. The scheme's autumn 2025 relaunch had been taken up within a single day, and its effect is visible in the registration data: EV share of the new-car market ran at 8.4% in the first half of 2026, above 10% in June, versus 5.2% a year earlier on Il Sole 24 ORE's numbers.

Two caveats for anyone building a business case on top of it. One, the scheme is lumpy: July's EV share fell back to 5.9%, and both UNRAE (the foreign manufacturers' association) and Motus-E (the e-mobility association) have publicly worried about the lack of a stable, predictable incentive — UNRAE's president has flagged the missing support for private BEV buyers specifically. Two, imported cars must still qualify: eligibility conditions, scrappage and ISEE documentation apply, and a parallel import outside a brand's official dealer channel may face additional hurdles we could not fully verify — treat specific eligibility as **UNKNOWN** and confirm with the dealer or MIMIT before promising a net price.

Below the border, the tax regime also leans pro-EV: no CO₂-based registration tax; provincial IPT registration fees commonly waived or sharply reduced for EVs; bollo exemption for five years then 25% of the standard rate; and a company-car fringe-benefit rate of 10% of the standard taxable amount for EVs (20% for PHEVs), against 50% for conventional cars — a quiet subsidy that makes Italy one of Europe's better markets for EV fleet adoption. The government has also earmarked €4.6 billion to support the domestic auto industry and fleet electrification. Our landed-cost model excludes all of this by design; it prices the car at the border, not the tax code around it.

## The PHEV loophole — and its likely closing

Look again at the matrix: the BYD Seal 06 DM-i lands at $25,138 (+79.6%), cheaper than several smaller BEVs, because plug-in hybrids pay the 10% duty and nothing else. That quirk is not an Italian policy; it is an EU-wide exemption, and it shows in Italian demand. Xinhua's reporting from BYD dealerships in Rome in August 2026 found plug-in hybrids "particularly popular because Italy's charging infrastructure is still limited," with the Atto 2 DM-i and Dolphin G DM-i drawing interest — and BYD claims more than one in four PHEVs sold in Italy.

The window may be closing. In June 2026, Handelsblatt reported — and Brussels-watchers relayed — that the European Commission was preparing countervailing duties on Chinese plug-in hybrids, targeting makers including BYD, Chery and SAIC, with an anti-subsidy investigation believed to be under way and the measure pending approval by member states. The mix shift behind the move is measurable: plug-in hybrids rose from 18% of Chinese brands' European volume in April 2025 to 30% in April 2026, and BYD became Germany's best-selling PHEV brand in May 2026. If a CVD lands on PHEVs at anything like the BEV rates, the arithmetic here changes fast: applying a 20.7% rate to the Seal 06 DM-i in our model would add roughly **$3,900** to its landing and push its premium from +79.6% to about +107%. That is a hypothetical, not a forecast — the timing, scope and rates were all undecided when this article was written, and we will update the model when a decision is published. But for anyone importing plug-in hybrids into Italy right now, it is the risk that sits under the whole spreadsheet.

## What buyers should check before committing

1. **Match the CVD to the brand — and the powertrain.** Rates run from 7.8% to 35.3% and apply to BEVs; PHEVs currently sit outside them. A brand's rate, not the car's size, often decides the deal: see our [BYD](/blog/byd-countervailing-duty-landed-cost/) and [Geely](/blog/geely-188-countervailing-duty/) rate explainers.
2. **Budget the fixed block honestly.** Certification and homologation are the big variables at ~$3,250 in our model before any surprises; a car without WVTA needs national approval — our [WVTA vs single-vehicle approval](/blog/wvta-vs-single-vehicle-approval/) piece maps the routes.
3. **Model VAT as a real cost.** 22% at import, no EV reduction; businesses should get tax advice on partial VAT deductibility (the standard rule limits input VAT on cars to 40%).
4. **Treat incentives as conditional.** The €6,000–€13,750 ecobonus and the bollo/fringe-benefit regime can move the total cost of ownership more than the duty does — but verify eligibility for your specific purchase channel before netting it off.
5. **Register where you will drive.** Italy's IPT/bollo regime applies on Italian registration; the EU's customs union kills cross-border VAT arbitrage.
6. **Watch three things.** The PHEV CVD decision; BYD's factory talks in Italy; and the localisation push (BYD's Hungary plant has started trial production; MG's European plant is reportedly headed for Spain). The more Chinese-brand cars are built in Europe, the less any of this table applies to them — and the more it applies only to pure imports like yours.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, Italy is the clearest case in Europe of why "importing a Chinese EV" has stopped being a pure tariff question: the country charges the full EU cost stack — including the highest big-market VAT in the EU — for cars it simultaneously wants Chinese manufacturers to build inside its own borders.

**Reasoning.** First, the numbers cut both ways here in an unusually visible manner: a BYD Seal lands at $45,117 (+82.7%) and the cheapest credible BEVs land at +102% to +147%, yet Italy's biggest EV sales push in years was driven by Chinese-developed models — roughly a quarter of all BEVs registered in the first seven months of 2026 were Leapmotor T03s sold through Stellantis, and Il Sole 24 ORE reports the eco-bonus itself "mainly favoured models produced by Chinese manufacturers." Second, the industrial politics point the same way: Stellantis' Italian output hit a 70-year low in 2025, and rather than close plants the group is assigning them "affordable EV" roles and deepening its Leapmotor joint venture in Spain, while BYD negotiates for underused Italian factories. Third, the one remaining cheap door into this market for Chinese brands — the PHEV exemption at 10% — is exactly the door Brussels was preparing to close in June 2026, and Italy is where that decision would bite hardest, because charging gaps make plug-in hybrids the pragmatic choice for many buyers. If I were underwriting an import position into Italy through 2027, I would price in CVD risk on PHEVs rather than assume the current arithmetic survives.

**Disclosure.** This is my interpretation of published rules, tariff data and press reporting — not legal, tax or customs advice, and not a prediction of the pending PHEV decision. The landed figures come from EV Hub's own model, which excludes incentives and is not a quote; eligibility details for the ecobonus on parallel imports remain partially unverified (flagged above). Confirm rates, origin treatment and registration with an Italian customs broker before committing capital.
