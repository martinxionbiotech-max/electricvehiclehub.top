## TL;DR

Two value imports, one size class apart, and the tightest landed-cost pair in this comparison series: the [Leapmotor C10](/vehicles/leapmotor-c10/) and the [BYD Atto 3](/vehicles/byd-atto-3/) finish **$1,113–$2,451 apart across seven markets** — **$33,933 vs $31,503** in Germany, **$23,161 vs $22,048** in Thailand. The C10 buys roughly 40% more battery and 284 mm more metal for $1,040 more at the factory gate, while Leapmotor's 20.7% EU countervailing rate — against BYD's 17.0% — quietly adds $838 of the German gap.

## Key statistics

- **C10 base price:** $17,300 China ex-factory — 69.9 kWh battery (52.9–81.9 kWh BEV variants), 530 km CLTC, 170 kW, 4,739 mm long
- **Atto 3 base price:** $16,260 China ex-factory — 49.92 kWh Blade battery, 430 km CLTC, 150 kW, 4,455 mm long
- **Germany:** C10 $33,933 (+96.1%) vs Atto 3 $31,503 (+93.7%) → **$2,431 gap**
- **France (widest gap):** $34,063 vs $31,612 → **$2,451**
- **Thailand (narrowest gap):** $23,161 vs $22,048 → **$1,113**
- **The rate difference:** Leapmotor carries the 20.7% "other cooperating" countervailing duty; BYD carries its sampled 17.0% — 3.7 points, worth $704 before VAT on the C10's duty-paid value, $838 once German VAT compounds over it
- **Outside the EU the gap is pure base price:** $1,040 ex-factory, compounded by local duty/VAT/GST into $1,113–$1,373 of landing difference
- **What the $1,040 buys:** +19.98 kWh (+40%), +284 mm of length, +105 mm of wheelbase — and +300 kg of curb weight against worse quoted efficiency (18.1 vs 12.2 kWh/100 km)

## The basis: ex-factory versus landed cost

**Ex-factory** is the price at the factory gate in China — $17,300 for the [C10](/vehicles/leapmotor-c10/), $16,260 for the [Atto 3](/vehicles/byd-atto-3/) (sold as the Yuan Plus at home). **Landed cost** is what an importer pays to bring each car through customs in the destination market: standard duty, countervailing duty where the EU applies it, and VAT or GST compounding in that order, plus a fixed block covering RoRo freight, customs clearance, certification, registration and inland transport.

Both columns in this comparison are the same kind of number — import-cost estimates, no margin inside — which makes this the cleanest subtraction in the series. The arithmetic still compounds rather than adds:

```
landed = base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs
```

Every figure below comes from the two EV Hub vehicle records, with the rate inputs documented in the [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

## The closest pair in the series

No segment debate here either: both are five-seat family SUVs pitched at value buyers, and the C10 is simply the bigger of the two — one long-wheelbase step up, with a battery to match.

Read the trade, not the badge. The C10 packs **19.98 kWh more battery** — 40% more — and 100 km more quoted range, yet gives 0.38 seconds back to the Atto 3 in the sprint and carries 300 kg more curb weight for the trouble. The Atto 3's quoted consumption (12.2 kWh/100 km) is about a third lower than the C10's 18.1. Both range figures are CLTC, which means they are comparable to each other and to nothing in the real world — the [cycle explainer](/blog/cltc-vs-wltp-range/) covers why. One structural note while the table is on screen: the C10's catalog also lists a 28 kWh EREV variant, and PHEV/EREV powertrains sit outside the EU's BEV countervailing-duty scope — a distinction we come back to below.

Note the premium-percentage crossover. Inside the EU the C10 wears the *heavier* premium (+96.1% vs +93.7% in Germany) — it pays a higher countervailing rate on a larger base. Dollars for decisions; percentages describe burden.

## Worked example: both cars through the same German customs office

| Line (Germany) | Leapmotor C10 | BYD Atto 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Base price (China ex-factory) | $17,300.00 | $16,260.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $1,730.00 | $1,626.00 |
| Countervailing duty (20.7% / 17.0%) | $3,939.21 | $3,040.62 |
| VAT (19%) | $4,364.15 | $3,976.06 |
| Fixed block (freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland) | $6,600.00 | $6,600.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$33,933.36** | **$31,502.68** |

Formulas, in order: `17,300 × 1.10 × 1.207 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 33,933.36` and `16,260 × 1.10 × 1.17 × 1.19 + 6,600 = 31,502.68`. The full rate stack — 10% standard duty, then the countervailing duty on the duty-paid value, then VAT on everything above — is the same for both cars. Only two inputs differ: the base price, and the rate the regulation assigns each producer.

## Where the money goes: $1,593 of car, $838 of rate

The $2,430.68 German gap decomposes cleanly, and the split matters for anyone negotiating a deal:

- **The base-price effect: $1,592.79.** Hold the CVD rate equal at Atto 3's 17% and the C10 lands at $33,095.47 — the $1,040 factory-gate difference, compounded through 10% duty and 19% VAT, explains this slice.
- **The rate effect: $837.89.** Leapmotor is not a sampled company in the EU's anti-subsidy case; it carries the 20.7% "other cooperating" default rate while BYD carries its individually assessed 17.0%. That 3.7-point spread is worth $704.11 on the C10's duty-paid value before VAT, and $837.89 once 19% compounds over it.

Outside the EU neither car pays any countervailing duty, and the gap collapses to pure base arithmetic — literally $1,040 passing through the local stack: ×1.10 → $1,144 in Australia; ×1.1025 → $1,146.60 in the UAE; ×1.07 → $1,112.80 in Thailand; ×1.10×1.20 → $1,372.80 in the UK; ×1.25 → $1,300 in Norway. There is no policy story in those five numbers at all; it is the same car-comparison the factory gate already settled, filtered once for tax.

## Where this comparison can go wrong

1. **Costs, not quotes, and not retail.** Both columns are importer-side estimates. Add your own margin, warranty reserve and resale assumptions before deciding; the house [margin math](/blog/fleet-buyer-margin-math/) piece walks through the frame.
2. **VAT is recoverable for registered importers.** It changes the cash requirement, not the underlying duty cost — and duty never comes back.
3. **Fixed costs are central estimates.** They are volume- and lane-sensitive; a one-off shipment lands above these numbers and a container programme lands below.
4. **The battery comparison is quoted, not measured.** CLTC figures flatter both cars; do not treat the 100 km quoted range delta as a real-world distance, and weigh the C10's extra 300 kg against it.
5. **Trim and powertrain scope.** The comparison here is the base BEV pair. If your plan involves the C10 EREV variant, note that plug-in hybrid powertrains are outside the EU's countervailing-duty scope (BEVs only) — a different transaction with a different stack, as our [PHEV tariff window](/blog/eu-phev-tariff-window/) piece covers.
6. **Rates move.** The countervailing regime is under periodic review; Leapmotor's "other cooperating" default rate is exactly the kind of input that can be reassigned. Model at current rates and stress the deal.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view, the C10-versus-Atto 3 decision is the most honest in this catalog precisely because it is so close: $1,113–$2,451 across seven markets for 40% more battery and a size class of extra metal. That is not a price war; it is a product choice with a clearly measurable surcharge — and the surcharge is smallest in exactly the markets where value importers do their volume.

### Reasoning

First, the decomposition does the arguing: strip the German gap down and $1,593 of it is the car itself, $838 is the regulatory rate Leapmotor carries versus BYD — so inside the EU, roughly a third of the premium buys you nothing at all. Second, outside the EU that third vanishes; the gap becomes the $1,040 factory difference passed through local tax and nothing else, which means an importer choosing between these two is choosing on the spec sheet, not on policy — a rare luxury in this dataset. Third, the spec trade cuts both ways: twenty extra kilowatt-hours and 284 mm of length are real, but so are +300 kg and a quoted consumption rate roughly 48% higher, and the C10 is even 0.38 s slower to 100 km/h. A fleet buyer doing urban duty cycles should price the Atto 3's efficiency advantage into the comparison; a family buyer sizing a second row should price the C10's wheelbase. Fourth, the sleeper fact in the records: the C10's EREV variant escapes the BEV-only countervailing regime entirely — if your market case needs the lowest possible EU duty exposure rather than the lowest factory price, that is a conversation worth having with a tariff broker before this one.
