## TL;DR

A China-built electric car enters Israel on a **7% customs duty, no countervailing duty and an 18% VAT** — a BYD Seal lands at about **$36,023 (+45.9%)**, roughly $8,400 less than the same car in Germany, because Israel sits outside the EU's countervailing-duty regime. The layer our border math deliberately excludes is the one that rules Israeli showroom prices: a **48% purchase tax on EVs** (up from 45%; the Finance Ministry's 52% bid was rejected in December 2025), still far below the up-to-83% rate on gasoline cars — and with the next formula signalled for January 2027.

## Key numbers

- **Import duty:** 7% on China-origin cars — a modest tariff, assessed on the CIF value
- **Countervailing duty:** none — Israel is not in the EU customs territory, so the EU's five-year anti-subsidy duty on China-built BEVs has no force there
- **VAT:** 18%, raised from 17% on 1 January 2025
- **Purchase tax on EVs:** 48% for 2026, assessed above a shekel-denominated benefit cut to NIS 22,000 (2025: 45% above NIS 35,000; the 52% proposal was rejected by the Knesset Finance Committee in December 2025)
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal, $24,690 ex-factory → **$36,023 landed (+45.9%)**; add an illustrative 48% purchase-tax layer and the practical retail-side floor moves north of $45,000
- **Cross-check (same Seal):** Germany $44,413 (+79.9%) · UK $38,840 (+57.3%) · Switzerland $33,807 (+36.9%) · UAE $31,670 (+28.3%) · Qatar $30,104 (+21.9%)
- **The real threshold:** not the tariff — the purchase tax, its shrinking shekel carve-out, and the January 2027 rewrite

## The terms that decide this market

- **Purchase tax** — Israel's car-specific consumption tax, and the single largest cost layer between a landed car and a retail price. It runs through the "green taxation" framework, so it is really two numbers: a headline rate (up to 83% on the most polluting gasoline cars; 48% for EVs today) and a shekel-denominated benefit that shields the first tranche of the car's value.
- **Green taxation** — the emissions-linked framework that sets purchase-tax rates in Israel. EVs sit in the most favourable band, but the band is re-cut almost every budget cycle: the EV rate has stepped 20% → 35% → 45% → 48% since 2023.
- **Customs duty** — levied at the border on the CIF value (car plus insurance and freight). Israel applies 7% on passenger cars in our records — low for a car tariff, and only the first of two border layers that apply.
- **Countervailing duty (CVD)** — an anti-subsidy tariff. The EU's version (17% to 35.3% by producer, on top of its 10% standard duty) applies only to imports into the EU customs territory. Israel is outside that perimeter, and no equivalent national measure is applied there in our records. This is the biggest single structural reason a Chinese EV lands cheaper at an Israeli port than at a German one.
- **VAT** — 18% at import, charged on the duty-paid value. It rose from 17% on 1 January 2025.
- **LHD** — Israel is a left-hand-drive market, so China-spec cars fit the road rules without conversion, unlike RHD markets such as the UK, Australia or New Zealand. For why that matters more than it sounds, see [LHD vs RHD: which markets can a Chinese EV actually serve?](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/).

## What a Chinese EV actually pays at the Israeli border

|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **7%** | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **None** — outside the EU regime | — | Yes (zero) |
| Purchase tax | **48%** (2026), above a NIS 22,000 benefit | duty-paid value (base to confirm) | **No — excluded** |
| VAT | **18%** | import value + duty | Yes |
| Registration & plates | NIS 5,000+ for EVs since 2025 (~$1,550); model-dependent | — | Yes — as a $700 line inside the $4,850 fixed block |

The headline looks moderate — a single-digit duty, no anti-subsidy tariff — and then purchase tax resets the conversation entirely. Everything above the purchase-tax line is border cost; the purchase tax is the domestic lever Israel actually uses, and it is where this market is won or lost.

That spread is the CVD-free border doing its work, and it is the cleanest illustration of why the same Chinese car has two very different price stories in two different regions.

### Worked example: a BYD Seal in Israel

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (7% of base) | $1,728 |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs` — with no CVD term, because there is none to apply. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 2026-09-15. For how the model is built, see [How our landed-cost model works](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

## Where this model stops: the purchase-tax layer

Treat $36,023 as a border number, not a showroom estimate. Our model captures duty and VAT but applies no purchase tax — and purchase tax is the number that rules Israeli pricing. Our Israel records list the band as 45–52%; that spread is not an error. It brackets the policy fight: 45% was the implemented 2025 rate, 52% was the Finance Ministry's proposed 2026 rate, and the eventual compromise set 48% from January 2026.

The rate has risen at every January reset, while the shekel benefit that sits under it has been cut in step:

| Year | EV purchase tax | Shekel benefit (carve-out on taxable value) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 20% | UNKNOWN | rate as reported mid-2023 |
| 2024 | 35% | NIS 50,000 | |
| 2025 | 45% | NIS 35,000 | registration levy and VAT also rose |
| 2026 | 48% | NIS 22,000 | 52% bid rejected; compromise of 29 Dec 2025 |
| Gasoline benchmark | up to 83% | — | the rate EVs are still compared against |

Two mechanics matter more than the headline rate. First, the tax is assessed on value above a shekel ceiling, so the benefit does not scale with price — "sources at the Ministry of Finance claim that the compromise will encourage the import of cheaper electric vehicles 'at the expense' of luxury electric vehicles, for which the tax benefit becomes negligible" ([Globes, 29 December 2025](https://en.globes.co.il/en/article-treasury-compromises-on-electric-car-tax-hike-1001530508)). Second, the effective burden on expensive EVs is therefore "much higher than the official" rate ([Globes, 12 December 2024](https://en.globes.co.il/en/article-tax-authority-confirms-higher-vehicle-tax-rates-for-2025-1001496683)) — an entry-level BYD Seagull gets real relief, a six-figure flagship gets almost none.

If a 48% rate were applied to the Seal's duty-paid value with the NIS 22,000 (about $6,800) carve-out, the layer adds roughly $9,400 and pushes the practical retail-side floor to about $45,400 before dealer margin. That is an order-of-magnitude illustration only: the statutory base for imported-car purchase tax, and its interaction with VAT, are mechanics we could not verify from primary sources (flagged UNKNOWN — confirm with a licensed Israeli importer or customs broker). The direction, though, is not in doubt. The purchase tax is several times the customs duty on the same car.

Two smaller 2025 changes point the same way: EV registration fees rose from a flat NIS 530 ($165) to more than NIS 5,000 ($1,550) depending on model, bringing EVs onto parity with gasoline cars, and VAT went from 17% to 18% ([The Times of Israel, 10 December 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-committee-rejects-treasury-bid-to-hike-electric-vehicle-purchase-tax-to-52/)). The market noticed: EV share of new-car purchases fell to 14% in the first half of 2025, from 25% over the same period in 2024, according to Energy Ministry figures cited in the same report.

One more signal for anyone underwriting a 2026–27 deal: Israeli financial press began previewing the next chapter in August 2026, with Ynetnews running "Car taxes are changing in Israel in 2027" and other Israeli outlets reporting a new green-tax formula circulated to importers for 1 January 2027. We could not retrieve the full texts from our environment — treat the 2027 specifics as UNKNOWN and re-confirm before contracting.

The $8,400 gap against Germany is almost entirely the countervailing duty plus Germany's higher VAT; the $2,800 gap against the UK is the VAT difference alone; and the Gulf's advantage over Israel is the 18% VAT that Qatar and the UAE don't charge at those levels. Israel's position — below every EU market, above every other Middle East stop we model — is exactly what its tax architecture predicts. ## The demand side: why Chinese brands own this market

Israel was one of the first export markets where Chinese EVs stopped being a novelty and became the default choice. The numbers from our sources:

- In November 2022, the BYD Atto 3 sold 2,333 units and was the best-selling model of any type in the country ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2023/07/10/xpeng-partners-with-freesbe-to-tap-israel-market/)).
- In April 2023, BYD delivered 1,377 EVs — about 40% of all EVs sold that month — and ranked fourth among all car brands with a 7.1% share ([CarNewsChina](https://carnewschina.com/2023/05/06/byd-continues-to-be-israels-best-selling-ev-brand-in-april-delivering-1377-evs/)).
- In the first three quarters of 2023, Chinese brands accounted for 62.2% of Israel's EV sales — 25,902 of 41,647 units — with BYD the best-selling EV brand at 13,481 cars, Geely second at 6,180, and Tesla third at 5,463 ([Xinhua, citing Israel Vehicle Importers Association data](https://english.news.cn/20231003/72fc819db83748ffba161811d1adab06/c.html)).

The distribution map followed the sales. XPeng partnered with Freesbe, one of Israel's largest dealer groups; Leapmotor had opened showrooms by late 2022; and Chinese brands kept arriving in waves through 2023 and beyond. For the volume-model mathematics behind this wave, see our [BYD volume EV landed-cost breakdown](/blog/byd-volume-ev-landed-cost/).

Two structural facts keep the demand story intact even as taxes rise. Israel's Energy Ministry plan calls for a complete ban on imports of gasoline and diesel vehicles from 2030, with imports limited to electric or gas vehicles. And the tax system is explicitly engineered to keep favouring EVs: 48% versus up to 83% is still a 35-point advantage. What has changed is the market's temperature — from one in four new cars being electric in early 2024 to roughly one in seven by mid-2025 — and as Globes noted in December 2025, large stocks of unsold EVs were still being discounted into the new year. A cooling market with heavy inventory is an importer's negotiating position, not a reason to stay home.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **The purchase-tax mechanics, not just the rate.** Confirm the statutory base and how the shekel benefit applies to your model (flagged UNKNOWN above). Model the 2027 formula risk as a cost escalation, not a tail event — the reset has gone up in three consecutive Januaries.
2. **Border estimate vs retail reality.** Israeli list prices carry purchase tax, logistics and thick distribution margins. In May 2023, when the EV purchase tax was still 20%, the Atto 3 listed from NIS 163,500 (~$45,400) against a China starting price of RMB 139,800 (~$20,100), per [CarNewsChina](https://carnewschina.com/2023/05/06/byd-continues-to-be-israels-best-selling-ev-brand-in-april-delivering-1377-evs/). Never benchmark a border number against a sticker.
3. **Structure for cheap models.** The tax design now favours lower-priced EVs by construction — the shekel carve-out is worth least on expensive cars. The Seagull/Dolphin class is where the math works best in this market.
4. **Countervailing duty is a non-issue — keep it that way on paper.** No CVD line exists for Israel, but the EU's regime applies to imports into the EU customs territory; if a car is imported into the EU first, EU rules apply to that import. See [How to import a Chinese EV to the EU](/blog/import-chinese-ev-eu-guide/) for the contrast.
5. **Distribution, homologation and service.** Chinese brands in Israel run through established local groups (XPeng via Freesbe, for example). Parallel importers should nail down model approval and service obligations before shipping — the same discipline as anywhere, but with Hebrew documentation and Israeli standard compliance in the loop.
6. **LHD fit and timing.** China-spec LHD vehicles suit Israel without conversion; [the LHD/RHD map](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) covers the filter. On timing: inventory is heavy and discounts are live, but the January 2027 tax reset is the deadline that matters.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, Israel is the clearest case in our 30-market data set of the tariff being the cheap part: a 7% duty, no countervailing duty, an 18% VAT — and a domestic purchase tax at 48% and climbing that decides whether any given model actually sells. I would underwrite an Israeli import play as a tax-escalation business case, not a tariff one.

**Reasoning.** First, the border structure is genuinely favourable and structural: because Israel sits outside the EU's countervailing-duty perimeter, a BYD Seal lands at +45.9% versus +79.9% in Germany, and no policy fight in Brussels can change that for an Israeli importer. Second, the domestic lever is being pulled steadily in one direction — 20% to 35% to 45% to 48% across four budget cycles, with the shekel benefit cut from NIS 50,000 to NIS 22,000, and a 2027 rewrite already signalled. Third, the tax design increasingly rewards exactly what Chinese exporters do best: affordable volume models, where the fixed carve-out still bites, rather than flagships where it is negligible. The 2024–25 demand slump (25% to 14% EV share) is real, but it is a price-point problem, not a product one — BYD and its peers still hold the top of the market.

**Disclosure.** This is my interpretation of published tax records, press reporting and EV Hub's landed-cost data — not tax, customs or investment advice. Several purchase-tax mechanics for imported cars remain unverified in our sources (flagged UNKNOWN above), and the January 2027 formula is not yet public in any form we could retrieve. Confirm rates, bases and timing with a licensed Israeli importer or customs broker before committing capital.
