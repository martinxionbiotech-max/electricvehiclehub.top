## TL;DR

The landed cost we've spent this whole series calculating is the *input* to a business decision, not the decision itself. Whether an import makes sense depends on **who you are and how much margin you need**:

- A **private importer** buying one car pays the full fixed-cost stack and needs the car to simply be worth it *to them* — no margin requirement, but zero cost-sharing.
- A **fleet buyer** can amortize some overhead across vehicles and cares about total cost of ownership, not just landed cost.
- A **dealer** needs a *spread* between landed cost and retail price that covers their margin, warranty, and after-sales — and that spread is the real make-or-break number.

The uncomfortable truth that separates the three: **a car that's a bargain for a private buyer can be a loss for a dealer**, because the dealer's margin requirement is a cost the other two don't have.

## Key statistics

- **Private importer:** pays full fixed costs (~$6,600) on one car; no margin requirement
- **Fleet buyer:** amortizes freight/overhead; cares about TCO and residual value
- **Dealer:** needs landed cost + margin + warranty + after-sales ≤ retail price
- **The deciding number for a dealer:** the spread between landed cost and what the market will pay
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal lands at $44,413 in Germany — a dealer needs retail headroom above that

## The three buyer types, and what they actually care about

| Buyer | Volume | Fixed-cost exposure | The number that matters | Margin requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Private importer | 1 | Full ($6,600 on one car) | "Is it worth it *to me*?" | None |
| Fleet buyer | 5–100+ | Amortized across units | Total cost of ownership | Indirect (resale value) |
| Dealer | 10+ | Amortized, but adds margin | Landed + margin ≤ retail | Yes — the decisive one |

The difference isn't the car — it's the *economics each buyer imposes on top of the landed cost*.

## The dealer's margin math (the one that decides)

For a dealer, the import only works if:

> **Landed cost + dealer margin + warranty/after-sales ≤ retail price the market will pay**

Take the BYD Seal landing at $44,413 in Germany. If a comparable European EV retails around $45,000, the dealer has almost no room — a few hundred dollars of margin before warranty and after-sales wipe it out. That's why the EU's countervailing duty matters so much: it eats exactly the margin a dealer needs.

Now take the same Seal landing in the UAE at $31,671. A dealer there has $13,000 of headroom before hitting the same retail price — a viable business. The tariff doesn't just change the buyer's price; it decides whether a *dealer* can operate at all.

## The fleet buyer's different lens

Fleet buyers don't think in "margin" — they think in **total cost of ownership (TCO)**: landed cost plus energy, maintenance, insurance, and residual value over the fleet life. For them:

- The landed cost is the *acquisition* line of a TCO spreadsheet, not the whole story.
- A car with a higher landed cost but a strong residual value and low running costs can still win.
- Volume lets them amortize freight and share certification/registration overhead across units.

Fleet buyers are also the most sensitive to the **RHD/LHD and charging-standard gates** we covered — a fleet decision is dead on arrival if the model isn't available in the right configuration or can't charge on the local network.

## The private importer's simple question

A private importer has no margin to defend — the only question is whether the landed cost is worth it *to them*. That makes the math simpler, but the risk higher: they absorb the full fixed-cost stack on a single car, and they bear the homologation and adaptation surprises alone. For a private buyer, the honest advice is usually: **buy the export version that's already homologated for your market**, rather than importing a China-market car yourself and discovering the compliance cost later.

## What to ask before committing (by buyer type)

**Private importer**
1. "Is the export version already homologated for my market?"
2. "Have I budgeted the full fixed-cost stack ($6,600) plus any adaptations?"

**Fleet buyer**
3. "What's the total cost of ownership, not just the landed cost?"
4. "Is the model available in my market's drive-side and charging standard?"

**Dealer**
5. "What's the spread between landed cost and the retail price my market will pay?"
6. "Does the countervailing duty leave room for my margin, warranty, and after-sales?"

## Frequently asked questions

**Which buyer type is a Chinese EV import best for?**
It depends entirely on the market. In low-tariff markets (UAE, Australia), the landed cost leaves room for dealer margin. In the EU, the countervailing duty often compresses the dealer's spread to near zero — making fleet or private use more viable than resale.

**Why can a bargain car be a bad dealer import?**
Because a dealer needs a spread between landed cost and retail price to cover margin, warranty, and after-sales. A car that's cheap to *own* isn't necessarily cheap to *resell* profitably.

**Does fleet volume change the landed cost?**
Some fixed costs (freight, clearance overhead) amortize across units, but certification and registration are largely per-vehicle. Volume helps, but it doesn't eliminate the fixed stack.

**What's the single biggest mistake buyers make?**
Running the landed-cost math last — after deciding on the car and market — instead of first. The drive-side, charging, and homologation gates decide *whether* you can import; the tariff decides *how much* it costs.
