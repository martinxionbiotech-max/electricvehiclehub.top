## TL;DR

Canada's **100% surtax on Chinese electric vehicles was repealed effective 1 March 2026**, dropping the charge on qualifying imports to the **6.1% MFN tariff plus 5% federal GST** — inside an annual quota of **49,000 vehicles** that grows toward roughly 70,000 over five years. A $24,690 BYD Seal lands at about **$34,546 (+39.9%)**, which makes Canada one of the most attractive developed markets in our catalog, on par with Australia. The catch is structural: the quota is roughly **3% of Canada's new-vehicle market**, so access — not tax — determines who can actually import.

## Key statistics

- **Surtax:** the 100% China surtax on EVs (China Surtax Order 2024, Sections 3 and Schedule 1) was **repealed effective 1 March 2026**
- **Import duty now:** **6.1% MFN** — the standard most-favoured-nation rate on passenger cars
- **GST:** **5% federal**; provinces add HST/PST on top, which our model does not include
- **Countervailing duty:** 0% — Canada applies no EU-style anti-subsidy tariff on Chinese EVs
- **Quota:** **49,000 units** initially, rising to about **70,000 within five years**; the government states this is under 3% of the Canadian new-vehicle market
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 → **$34,546 in Canada (+39.9%)**, versus $33,009 Australia (+33.7%) and $50,011 Mexico (+102.6%)
- **The real threshold:** **quota allocation**, not tax. The 6.1% rate applies to eligible imports within the managed volume

## Defining the change, because the headline number is no longer 100%

For roughly fifteen months, Canada was effectively closed to Chinese EVs. Following the United States, Ottawa imposed a **100% surtax** on China-origin electric vehicles in 2024, layered on top of the standard tariff. On a $24,690 car that meant an extra $24,690 payable at the border before any tax — an import-killing number.

That regime ended. Under the **Canada–China strategic partnership announced on 16 January 2026**, Canada committed to a managed import quota, and in consequence the 100% surtax was repealed to ensure eligible imports are subject only to the **MFN tariff of 6.1%**. The Canada Border Services Agency confirmed the repeal on 26 February 2026, effective 1 March 2026, and advised importers to amend declarations and claim refunds for surtax already paid.

Two things are worth stating plainly. First, the 6.1% rate is not a new preferential rate invented for China — it is Canada's ordinary MFN tariff, the same rate applied to most passenger-car origins. Second, the repeal is tied to the quota framework: the volume and conditions are defined by the Import Control List amendment, not by the tariff itself.

Canada sits a whisker above Australia — about $1,537 on the Seal — and dramatically below Mexico, where a 50% duty and 16% VAT push the same car past $50,000. Relative to the pre-repeal regime, the change is not a few percentage points; it removed a 100% charge that made the market unworkable.

Read the Canada column with one adjustment: it includes the **federal 5% GST only**. Canadian provinces add their own HST or PST, so the true out-the-door cost is higher than the modelled figure. The model is a floor, not a quote.

## The quota is the real gate, and it is rationed

Here is where the Canadian market differs from Australia or Switzerland. Canada did not simply cut the tariff; it created a **managed import volume**. The initial quota of **49,000 units** is calibrated to recent import volumes before the surtax, and Ottawa frames it as under 3% of the national new-vehicle market. The quota is set to grow toward about **70,000 vehicles within five years**.

That design has consequences:

- **Access is allocated, not automatic.** A tariff is a tax anyone can pay; a quota is a permission that some importers hold and others do not. The economic advantage flows to whoever holds allocation.
- **The volume is small relative to demand.** 49,000 units against a 1.7-million-unit market means Canada can be simultaneously cheap and inaccessible.
- **Policy risk is symmetric.** The same mechanism that opened the market can close it. A quota with a defined volume and a review horizon can be narrowed at the review, whereas a tariff cut is politically harder to reverse quickly.
- **Compliance still applies.** Transport Canada registration and provincial licensing are unchanged by the trade deal. Shipping a car into Canada is not the same as being allowed to sell it there.

## What buyers should ask before importing to Canada

1. **"Do I hold quota allocation, or am I buying from someone who does?"** — this is the first question, ahead of any tax math.
2. **"Is the declaration under the amended Customs Notice?"** — CBSA advised importers to update declarations and claim **refunds** for surtax paid before 1 March 2026. If you paid the old rate, you may be owed money.
3. **"Which province, and what does it add?"** — our 5% is federal GST only; HST/PST changes the answer materially.
4. **"Is the model Transport Canada compliant?"** — the tariff repeal does not create a compliance exemption.
5. **"What happens at the quota review?"** — model the downside, because the volume is a policy variable, not a constant.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Canada still charge 100% on Chinese EVs?**
No. The 100% surtax was repealed effective 1 March 2026. Qualifying imports now pay the 6.1% MFN tariff within an annual quota of 49,000 vehicles.

**What is Canada's total tax on a Chinese EV now?**
6.1% MFN duty plus 5% federal GST. Provinces add HST/PST separately, which our model does not include.

**Is there a quota on Chinese EV imports to Canada?**
Yes. The initial volume is 49,000 units per year, rising toward about 70,000 within five years, established under the Import Control List amendment that accompanied the surtax repeal.

**Can I claim a refund on surtax I already paid?**
The Canada Border Services Agency advised importers to amend Commercial Accounting Declarations and request refunds for surtax paid before the repeal took effect. Confirm the procedure and deadlines with CBSA or a customs broker.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view the Canadian surtax repeal is the single biggest tariff reversal of the year for Chinese EVs — but it creates a **rationed** market, not an open one, and the importer who wins is the one holding quota, not the one with the cheapest supplier.

**Reasoning.** First, the arithmetic genuinely works now: at 6.1% plus 5% GST, Canada lands a BYD Seal at $34,546, within about $1,500 of Australia and roughly $16,000 below Mexico, which is a remarkable turnaround from a 100% wall. Second, the quota design means the benefit is captured at the allocation stage; 49,000 units against 1.7 million annual sales is real volume but far short of demand, so the margin will sit with whoever can get cars counted inside the quota. Third, I would treat the 6.1% as a policy variable rather than a settled rate — it exists because of a bilateral arrangement with a review horizon, and importers who model it as permanent are taking a position on Canadian politics they may not intend to take. If I were underwriting a Canada import today, I would size the deal to the quota I control and assume the rate could move at the next review.
