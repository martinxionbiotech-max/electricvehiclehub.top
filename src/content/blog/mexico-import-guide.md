## TL;DR

Since 1 January 2026, Chinese vehicles entering Mexico face a **50% import duty plus 16% IVA**. A BYD Seal priced at $24,690 ex-factory China lands at about **$50,011 (+102.6%)** — the highest landed cost for that car across all 30 markets EV Hub tracks, above Denmark and well above Germany. The threshold is no longer price; it is **assembly location**. CBU imports pay the wall, locally built cars do not.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty:** 50% — the top rate in Mexico's general import tariff law, applied to passenger cars from countries without an FTA with Mexico
- **Previous rate:** 20% before the decree; the increase landed on 1 January 2026
- **VAT (IVA):** 16%, applied on the duty-paid value
- **Countervailing duty:** none — this is a tariff, not an anti-subsidy measure
- **Scope:** 1,463 tariff lines across vehicles, parts, steel, textiles, appliances and more; auto parts were hit alongside finished cars
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal ($24,690 base) → **$50,011 landed in Mexico (+102.6%)**
- **The real hurdle:** tariff avoidance now requires local assembly, not better freight or a smarter customs broker

## What changed on 1 January 2026

Mexico's tariff package was approved by the Senate in December 2025 and published in the DOF on 29 December 2025, taking effect on 1 January 2026. The Secretaría de Economía's own count is 1,463 tariff fractions. Cars carry the top rate — 50% — whether they run on petrol, diesel or a battery. There is no EV carve-out.

That last point is the one that matters most to Chinese brands. China was the biggest single source of vehicles into Mexico before the decree, and Mexico was China's largest single export destination for vehicles. The tariff was not aimed at a niche; it was aimed at the mainstream of the trade.

Mexico also levied the same package on auto parts. That has a second-order effect that importers tend to miss: higher duty on collision parts feeds directly into repair estimates, and repair cost is the primary input insurers use to set physical-damage premiums. A low-volume import with one distributor and no domestic parts depot gets longer cycle times after a crash and, eventually, higher comprehensive premiums.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in Mexico

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (50% of base) | $12,345 |
| IVA (16% of base + duty) | $5,926 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $7,050 |
| **Total landed** | **$50,011** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Inputs come from EV Hub's tariff records and the corresponding model's landed-cost file, both dated 2026-09-15.

One honest caveat: the fixed-cost block is the residual between the published formula and the published total, so it bundles RoRo freight, clearance, certification, registration and inland transport rather than itemizing them per market. The tax lines are exact; the logistics block is aggregated.

## The counter-argument worth reading: the tariff has not stopped the sales

Chinese brands held roughly **17% of Mexico's new light-vehicle market in the first half of 2026**, up from 14% a year earlier. At the same time, imports of Chinese light vehicles fell about 43% by value in the first five months of 2026 compared with 2025.

Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the whole story. Pre-tariff inventory was still moving through dealer lots. That buffer is finite. Once it clears, sticker prices have to reflect the duty, and the arithmetic above becomes the actual transaction price rather than a customs-broker's worksheet.

## The real threshold: build here or don't come

Mexico's tariff is a location decision dressed up as a tax.

- **CBU imports:** 50% duty, no route around it, plus 16% IVA. The jump-pad thesis — ship to Mexico, sell into North America — is dead at these rates.
- **Local assembly:** a vehicle built in Mexico is not an import and does not carry the 50%. This is why the tariff package is best read as industrial policy, not fiscal policy. It converts a trading relationship into an investment requirement.
- **Parts strategy:** because components were tariffed too, an assembler's local-content plan now determines both build cost and after-sales cost. That is a much bigger commitment than a distribution deal.

For a distributor weighing a first order, the honest question is no longer "what is the landed cost?" It is "do we have, or can we get, an assembly programme in Mexico — and if not, why are we in this market at all?"

## What buyers should check before committing

- **Is your tariff line confirmed at 50%?** The package moved 1,463 fractions; verify the specific NCM code rather than assuming the headline rate.
- **How much pre-tariff inventory is still in the channel?** Channel stock prices are not representative of post-tariff economics.
- **What is your parts and service plan?** With components tariffed, this line item changes the total cost of ownership more than the duty does for some models.
- **Is local assembly on the roadmap?** If not, the CBU business has a defined shelf life.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I think the 50% Mexican duty killed the CBU jump-pad story outright, and anyone still pitching Mexico as a cheap route into North America is selling a pre-2026 spreadsheet.

**Reasoning:** First, the number itself is decisive — a BYD Seal at $50,011 landed is more expensive than importing the same car into Germany, a market with a 10% tariff and a 17% countervailing duty, which should be impossible to say about a nearshoring destination. Second, the interim sales data cuts the other way and I want to be fair to it: Chinese brands gained share to 17% while the tariff was live, so the market did not collapse. But that strength was built on pre-tariff inventory, and inventory is a timing effect, not a business model. Third, the parts tariff is the underrated part of the package — it raises the cost of keeping an imported car on the road, which is exactly how a tariff turns a distribution business into a liability.

**Disclosure:** This is my opinion, not a customs ruling. Rates, scope and exemptions should be confirmed against the current DOF text and a Mexican customs broker.
