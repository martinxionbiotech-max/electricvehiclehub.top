## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV into Poland costs the full EU tariff stack: **10% standard duty, a brand-specific countervailing duty of 17–35.3%, and 23% VAT** — a rate only the Nordic markets exceed in our EU data. A BYD Seal lands at about **$45,240 (+83.2%)**, roughly $826 dearer than the same car in Germany. What makes Poland matter anyway is volume: it set an all-time record with 667,578 new-car registrations in 2025, Chinese brands hit a record 14.5% monthly share in December, and the subsidy pipeline that softened the math — NaszEauto, up to PLN 40,000 per car — is now fully allocated.

## Key numbers

- **Standard import duty:** 10% (EU most-favoured-nation rate) on China-origin passenger cars
- **Countervailing duty (CVD):** brand-specific — BYD 17%, Geely and its brands 18.8%, SAIC/MG 35.3%, other cooperating producers ~20.7% (our working default). BEVs only, for now
- **VAT:** 23%, charged on CIF + duty + CVD
- **Excise (akcyza):** BEVs are fully exempt; petrol cars pay 3.1% (≤ 2.0 L) or 18.6% (> 2.0 L), hybrids 1.55% / 9.3%
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal, $24,690 ex-factory → **$45,240 landed (+83.2%)** — the fifth-highest landing of the twelve EU markets we model
- **Same Seal elsewhere:** Germany $44,413 (+79.9%) · France $44,631 (+80.8%) · UK $38,841 (+57.3%) · UAE $31,671 (+28.3%) · Thailand $31,068 (+25.8%)
- **Subsidy status:** NaszEauto budget utilisation passed 100% on 27 January 2026; the intake window ran only to 30 April 2026
- **Gateway fact:** the Małaszewicze rail complex handles an estimated 85–90% of freight trains entering the EU from China

## The terms that decide this market

- **Landed cost** — the all-in cost of putting a car on a Polish registration desk: ex-factory price + freight + insurance + duty + CVD + VAT + clearance + certification + registration + inland transport. It is the number the showroom sticker has to cover, and the first thing private buyers and importers get wrong.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance, freight: the customs value the 10% duty is calculated on.
- **Countervailing duty (CVD)** — the EU's anti-subsidy tariff on China-built BEVs, set per producer under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 and levied on top of the standard duty. It follows the manufacturer, not the port: a BYD pays 17% whether the car clears in Gdańsk or Hamburg.
- **MFN duty** — the EU's common external tariff: 10% on passenger cars, charged before CVD and VAT.
- **Akcyza** — Poland's national excise tax on cars, declared within 14 days of import. BEVs are exempt, but the paperwork is mandatory: you cannot register the car without the excise receipt or exemption certificate.
- **VAT recoverability** — the 23% is charged at import for everyone, but VAT-registered businesses can usually recover it later. That distinction changes the true cost by nearly a fifth.
- **WVTA / single-vehicle approval** — EU type approval versus national approval for a car not built to an EU approval. The route you pick sets certification cost and lead time before the car even ships.

If you want the mechanics of the three-layer stack — why 10 + 17 + 23 does not equal 50 — we worked through it in the [EU import guide](/blog/import-chinese-ev-eu-guide/).

## What a Chinese EV actually pays at the Polish border

| Layer | Rate for a China-origin BEV | Levied on | In EV Hub's landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **10%** (MFN) | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **17–35.3%**, by brand | CIF + duty | Yes |
| Excise (akcyza) | **0% for BEVs** | duty-paid value | n/a (zero) |
| VAT | **23%** | CIF + duty + CVD | Yes |
| Registration & plates | **~$55** in our records | — | Yes |

The stack compounds rather than adds. For a BYD (17% CVD), the multiplier is 1.10 × 1.17 × 1.23 = **1.58**, so duty and tax alone lift the CIF price by about 58% before a single fixed cost. For a SAIC-built MG (35.3%), it is 1.10 × 1.353 × 1.23 = **1.83** — an 83% uplift before freight. This is the arithmetic almost every rough "tariff" estimate gets wrong, and it is exactly why the [SAIC/MG position](/blog/saic-35-percent-countervailing-duty/) is so punishing at the budget end of the market.

One live variable: the EU is preparing to extend countervailing duties to plug-in hybrids — an investigation was under way as of June 2026 — so the "BEVs only" carve-out that PHEV importers have enjoyed since 2024 may close. As of our data cut, register 2026-09-15, our model applies CVD to BEVs only.

Three patterns stand out, and all three matter for how you position an import.

First, the premium runs inverse to price. The Wuling MINI EV more than triples (+206.2%) because duty and taxes are percentages while the $6,155 fixed block is not — on a $5,000 car, the fixed costs alone are 123% of base. At the other end, the $61,830 Zeekr 009 carries the lowest premium in the group (+70.7%). This is the same "expensive percentage of a cheap car" mechanism we documented across every high-cost market, and Central Europe is no exception.

Second, the brand CVD can override the price ladder. The MG 4 pays SAIC's 35.3% and lands at +146.6% — worse than dearer cars — while the similarly priced Leapmotor T03 (20.7% CVD) lands at +136.2%. Two cars with the same China price tag can differ by a few thousand dollars in Warsaw purely because of who built them.

Third, the ranking is not strictly by price: the Xiaomi SU7 (+83.6%) lands proportionally worse than the cheaper BYD Seal (+83.2%) because Xiaomi sits at the 20.7% default rate while BYD pays 17%. If your comparison spreadsheet sorts by "cheapest to import" using price alone, it is sorting by the wrong column. For how the fixed block is built, see [How our landed-cost model works](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in Poland

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (10%) | $2,469 |
| Countervailing duty (17% of CIF + duty) | $4,617 |
| VAT (23% of CIF + duty + CVD) | $7,308 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $6,155 |
| **Total landed** | **$45,240** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 2026-09-15.

Now the comparison everyone in Poland should actually run — against Germany. The same Seal lands at $44,413 in Germany (+79.9%). Poland's landing is **$826 higher**, and the cause is one line item: VAT. Germany's 19% VAT adds $6,037 on this car; Poland's 23% adds $7,308 — a difference of $1,271. Poland's cheaper registration ($55 versus $500) gives back $445, but that does not cover the gap. Where Western European importers treat VAT as the minor final layer, in Poland it is the single biggest tax line after the car itself.

| Sweden | 10% | 17% | 25% | $45,890 | +85.9% |
| United Kingdom | 10% | none | 20% | $38,841 | +57.3% |
| UAE | 5% | none | 5% | $31,671 | +28.3% |
| Thailand | 0% | none | 7% | $31,068 | +25.8% |

Within the EU, the duty and CVD are identical everywhere — the only country-level variable is VAT, and Poland's 23% puts it among the bloc's most expensive landings for this car, ahead of Germany, France and the Netherlands, behind only Denmark, Sweden, Portugal and Ireland in our twelve EU markets. Outside the EU, there is no countervailing duty at all — a line worth roughly $5,500 on this car once the VAT it would attract is counted. The UK lands a Seal at $38,841, and Gulf or ASEAN markets closer to $31,000; none of them applies the EU's CVD. The [Germany–Netherlands–France comparison](/blog/germany-netherlands-france-import-comparison/) covers why VAT is normally a rounding error inside the EU; Poland is the exception that proves the rule.

## Why the volume still matters more than the margin

The landed-cost math is harsh, and the market data says importers keep coming anyway. In 2025 Poland registered **667,578 new cars** — the highest annual total in the country's history, beating the 1999 record — with December alone up 21.6% year on year. Chinese brands ended 2025 at **8.2% of the market**, and in December they took a record **14.5% monthly share**, nearly doubling their November volume. Jaecoo and MG models were among the country's most-registered newcomers.

The trend carried into 2026. First-quarter registrations reached **151,600 passenger cars, up 6.7%** — still running ahead of the European average — and the used-car import channel that long defined Poland's market is shrinking as competitively priced new Chinese models replace it. On OTOMOTO, the country's dominant marketplace, Chinese brands' share of new-car listings rose from 4.8% to **13.4%** in a year, and the platform now lists offers from about 40 Chinese manufacturers, up from 27. The average price of a new Chinese car fell 5.2% year on year to **PLN 139,069 (≈ $36,950)** — evidence that the segment is no longer just budget microcars; it now competes through the mid-market.

That is the "volume-price hub" thesis in one sentence: Poland has become the biggest and most competitive stage for Chinese cars in Central Europe, even though each unit lands more expensively than in Germany. Fleets explain part of the resilience — BEVs in the Polish long-term rental fleet grew around 103% year on year in Q1 2026 — and price competition explains the rest. But there is one support beam that has clearly been removed.

## NaszEauto: the subsidy chapter is closed

For two years, Poland's purchase subsidy was the counterweight to the VAT rate: **NaszEauto** launched on 3 February 2025 with subsidies up to **PLN 40,000 (≈ $10,600)** per passenger car, a base amount of PLN 18,750, and top-ups for scrapping an old combustion car. The vehicle price cap was PLN 225,000 net — wide enough to cover most China-built models. Notably, the original edition was aimed at individuals and sole proprietorships, not companies.

The programme was expanded in the autumn — from 20 October 2025 it added delivery vehicles and minibuses, with up to PLN 70,000 for vans — but the money ran out faster than the rules could adapt. By 12 November 2025 the ministry reported more than half of the allocation used and over 19,500 beneficiaries; by **27 January 2026 budget utilisation passed 100%**. The intake window closed at 30 April 2026 or when funds were exhausted, and by 10 September 2026 all applications submitted in the intake had been taken up for verification.

For anyone building an import case today: treat subsidy as zero. Whether a successor programme gets funded is **UNKNOWN** at the time of writing. If you were counting on PLN 40,000 to cover the VAT gap, that plan needs rewriting — the math above assumes no subsidy, and that is now the only safe assumption.

## The land gateway: what Małaszewicze means for Poland's role

Poland's pull for Chinese brands is not only demand. It is geography. The **Małaszewicze** rail complex on the Belarus border — a dual-gauge transshipment hub where the European standard gauge meets the 1,520 mm broad gauge — handles an estimated **85–90% of freight trains entering the EU from China**. When the border closed for two weeks in September 2025, more than 100 freight trains were blocked and a corridor carrying trade worth roughly €25 billion a year was disrupted — a reminder that the land route's advantage and its fragility are the same fact.

Warsaw is investing to keep the gateway: the terminal operator Cargotor was transferred to national rail infrastructure manager PKP PLK under a December 2025 agreement, and a modernisation programme worth PLN 3–4 billion (≈ €700–930 million) is planned for the Małaszewicze area. Add Poland's Baltic ports and you have a country that sits on both the land and sea legs of the China–Europe route — the trade spine that keeps Chinese OEMs committed to the region even when the tariff math turns against individual units.

The politics are less friendly than the geography. In the October 2024 member-state vote on the EU's countervailing duties, **Poland voted in favour** — alongside France and Italy, with Germany, Hungary and Slovakia against. Poland wanted the tariffs. Importers should expect active enforcement of the CVD regime in Polish ports and customs offices, not a soft touch.

And the competitive clock is ticking from the supply side: BYD's Szeged plant in neighbouring Hungary, its first European passenger-car factory, is now expected to start production in late 2026 — about a year later than originally planned. Once that plant and its peers ramp, EU-built Chinese EVs will skip the entire duty-plus-CVD stack (intra-EU trade pays no import duty). For a Polish importer, that turns "how do I land a Shanghai-built car" into "when does my brand localise" — a different business model entirely. Until then, every number on this page assumes a China-built car, and that is the right frame for 2026 planning.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **Confirm your brand's CVD rate** against Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 — 17% (BYD), 18.8% (Geely group), 35.3% (SAIC/MG), ~20.7% default for other cooperating producers. Then check the model's powertrain: only BEVs are in scope today, but PHEV coverage is being prepared.
2. **Run the VAT scenario both ways.** For a VAT-registered business the 23% is largely recoverable; for a private buyer it is real, permanent cost. The same car has two very different economics.
3. **Get the excise paperwork right.** BEVs are exempt, but registration requires the excise receipt or exemption certificate (AKC-U filing, 14-day deadline). Do not ship before this is understood.
4. **Budget the fixed block honestly.** $6,155 in our Polish model — and $3,250 of that is compliance and certification. It is the reason cheap cars land badly, and it does not scale down.
5. **Check the homologation route** before purchase: existing EU type approval, or single-vehicle approval. Poland is a left-hand-drive, CCS2 market, so a China-built car needs no RHD conversion — but it does need a compliant plug, paperwork and approval. Our [LHD vs RHD primer](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) and [CCS2 vs GB/T explainer](/blog/ccs2-vs-gbt-charging/) cover the two most common mismatches.
6. **Do not model subsidies into the unit economics.** The programme is closed; a successor is unannounced.
7. **Watch the corridor.** Rail disruption (as in September 2025) reroutes cargo and adds weeks; sea freight is the alternative leg, and our model's $2,000 RoRo line reflects that normal route.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, Poland is the most misunderstood market in this entire data set: it has the demand profile of a growth market and the landed-cost profile of a high-tax one, and the importers who win here will be the ones who price the second part correctly. The 23% VAT makes Poland one of the most expensive places in the EU to land a Chinese EV — and that is precisely why it has also become the most fiercely price-competitive Chinese-brand market in Central Europe.

**Reasoning.** First, the arithmetic is unforgiving: a Seal lands $826 higher in Poland than in Germany, all of it VAT, and no subsidy remains to offset it — the NaszEauto fund crossed 100% utilisation in January 2026. Second, the volume is real and structural: 667,578 registrations in 2025 (an all-time record), Chinese brands at a record 14.5% share in December, ~40 Chinese marques listing cars, and a fleet channel growing BEV penetration at triple-digit rates. Third, the market is maturing from "cheapest microcar" to "mid-market competitor" — the average new Chinese car now lists around PLN 139,069 — which shifts the profit question from import arbitrage to distribution, aftersales and fleet economics. Meanwhile the corridor and the political setting (Poland voted for the tariffs) mean logistics reliability and compliance are the moats, not tariff loopholes.

**Disclosure.** This is my interpretation of published rules, tariff data and press reporting — not legal, tax or customs advice. Two caveats: excise practice for imported cars is drawn from professional tax-practice sources rather than the statute itself, and whether the EU's PHEV tariff extension lands, and when, remains undecided. Confirm the current rates and your own VAT position with a Polish customs broker before committing capital.
