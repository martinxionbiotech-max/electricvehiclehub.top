## TL;DR

Australia completes our 30-market import-guide matrix, and on the numbers it is one of the openest large markets in the set: **0% import duty, no countervailing duty, and 10% GST**, which lands a $24,690 BYD Seal at **$33,009 (+33.7%)** — the fourth-cheapest result of our 30 markets and about **$11,404 below the same car in Germany**. What decides an Australian import is not the tax stack: you need a **factory right-hand-drive export variant**, and the car must clear **ADR compliance and RAV entry** before it can be registered.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty: 0.0%** — Australia's standard duty for this lane is zero in our tariff records (effective 7 September 2026, last verified 15 September 2026)
- **Countervailing duty: none** — our CVD measure is EU-only; the UK, Gulf and Australia pay no anti-subsidy duty on Chinese BEVs
- **GST: 10%** — the only consumption tax at the border, lower than every European VAT rate in our data except Switzerland's 8.1%, and recoverable for GST-registered businesses
- **Fixed costs — Oceania basket: $5,350** (freight, clearance, certification, inland transport) **plus a $500 registration fee**
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 → **$33,009.00 landed (+33.7%)**; the same car lands at $33,766.50 in New Zealand (+36.8%), $38,840.80 in the UK (+57.3%), $44,413.48 in Germany (+79.9%)
- **Market rank:** Australia is the fourth-cheapest of our 30 markets for the Seal — only Qatar ($30,104.50), Thailand ($31,068.30) and the UAE ($31,670.70) land it lower
- **The Oceania crossover:** Australia and New Zealand flip at **$9,540 ex-factory** — below it New Zealand lands cheaper (BYD Seagull: $15,654.00 vs $15,684.00); above it Australia wins (Seal: $757.50 cheaper; Zeekr 001: $1,423.00 cheaper)
- **The pricing shape:** premiums in our Australia table run from +75.4% on the $8,940 BYD Seagull down to +25.4% on the $38,000 Zeekr 001 — the fixed basket dominates the small end, as in every market we cover
- **The gate:** ADR compliance and RAV entry decide what is registrable, the fleet-average NVES CO2 targets took effect 1 January 2025 (enforced from 1 July 2025), and the RHD filter cuts the catalog down to the export builds a brand actually ships
- **UNKNOWN in our records:** the legal basis of the 0% duty (our dataset does not state whether the China–Australia FTA or another instrument is responsible) and the Luxury Car Tax, which our landed-cost model does not include

## The terms that decide an Australian import

| Term | What it means for an Australian import |
|---|---|
| **GST** | Australia's 10% goods and services tax, charged at the border on the import value. Recoverable for GST-registered businesses, a final cost for private buyers. |
| **Landed cost** | Base price ex-factory China, plus duty, countervailing duty, GST and fixed costs. Our [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/) documents the formula; every figure in this article comes from it. |
| **ADR (Australian Design Rules)** | Australia's national vehicle standards framework. Our regulatory records name ADR 79 (emissions) and ADR 81/02 (energy consumption label) among the rules a vehicle must satisfy for approval; the item-by-item ADR mapping and latest amendments are UNKNOWN in those records. |
| **RAV (Register of Approved Vehicles)** | The register a model must enter before it can be registered for Australian roads. ADR conformity is the precondition, and a China-market car does not arrive carrying it. |
| **RHD (right-hand drive)** | Australia drives on the left, so the routine import path runs on factory right-hand-drive vehicles. China's domestic lineup is left-hand drive — the RHD export build is a subset of each brand's catalog. |
| **NVES** | The New Vehicle Efficiency Standard Act 2024: fleet-average CO2 intensity targets for passenger cars and light commercial vehicles, effective 1 January 2025 and enforced from 1 July 2025, with penalties recorded around A$100 per g/km and tradable or carry-forward credits. |

## The tax stack: 0% duty, 10% GST, no countervailing duty

Australia's border position is the cleanest in our 30-market dataset. The standard duty rate is **0.0%**, there is **no countervailing duty** — the EU's producer-specific rates (BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%) apply to BEVs entering the EU, not to Australia — and GST is **10%**. With both duty layers at zero, GST is charged on the bare import value: nothing compounds underneath it.

That structure separates Australia from almost every other developed market we track:

| Line | Australia | New Zealand | United Kingdom | Germany (EU example) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard import duty | 0% | 0% | 10% | 10% |
| Countervailing duty | none | none | none | 17–35.3% (BEV, producer-specific) |
| Consumption tax | GST 10% | GST 15% | VAT 20% | VAT 19% |
| Registration fee (model input) | $500 | $23 | $400 | $500 |
| BYD Seal ($24,690 base) | **$33,009.00 (+33.7%)** | $33,766.50 (+36.8%) | $38,840.80 (+57.3%) | $44,413.48 (+79.9%) |

Read across the Seal row and the entire story of this market is visible: Australia and New Zealand share the same open border and differ only in consumption tax and registration; the UK sits $5,831.80 above Australia, the price of its 10% duty and 20% VAT; and Germany sits $11,404.48 above, because the EU's countervailing duty stacks on top of its standard tariff and drags VAT up with it.

Two gaps in our records are worth naming before you model anything. First, the dataset records the **0.0% rate** but not the instrument behind it — whether the China–Australia FTA, an MFN zero, or another measure provides the duty-free path is UNKNOWN here. Second, the **Luxury Car Tax** does not appear anywhere in our landed-cost records, so none of the numbers in this article include it; treat LCT treatment as UNKNOWN and confirm it directly before committing capital.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal lands in Australia

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690.00 |
| Standard import duty (0%) | $0.00 |
| Countervailing duty (none) | $0.00 |
| GST (10% of the import value) | $2,469.00 |
| Fixed costs — Oceania basket (freight, clearance, certification, inland transport) | $5,350.00 |
| Registration fee | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$33,009.00 (+33.7%)** |

The formula: `landed = base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. For Australia: `24,690 × 1.10 + 5,850 = 33,009.00`. The rates compound in that order — duty, then countervailing duty, then consumption tax — which is where cross-border quotes go wrong when a market has all three layers; here only the GST layer is live, so the compounding question is simple. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 15 September 2026, and the result is a model estimate rather than a quotation. Read every landed figure as cash-at-the-border, not sticker — [the gap between a quote and a registered car](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/) is where import plans usually break. If you are GST-registered, the $2,469.00 GST line is typically recoverable, which is why B2B and private buyers should read this table differently.

## Australia vs New Zealand: the Oceania crossover at $9,540

The two Oceania markets are the closest pair in our dataset — same open border, same fixed basket base, same absence of countervailing duty — so the entire difference reduces to two lines: consumption tax (Australia 10%, New Zealand 15%) and the registration fee (Australia $500, New Zealand $23). New Zealand's registration is $477 cheaper; Australia's GST is five points lower. Write the two formulas — `AU = base × 1.10 + 5,850` and `NZ = base × 1.15 + 5,373` — and they cross at a China base price of **$9,540**:

- **BYD Seagull ($8,940):** New Zealand wins by $30.00 ($15,654.00 vs $15,684.00) — below the crossover, the registration-fee gap outweighs the GST gap.
- **MG 4 ($9,690):** Australia wins by $7.50 — the closest result on the table, sitting right on the crossover.
- **BYD Atto 3 ($16,260):** Australia wins by $336.00; **BYD Seal:** by $757.50; **Zeekr 001:** by $1,423.00.

So the Oceania answer is: buy the top of the range in Australia, and the microcar end is the only place New Zealand lands cheaper. It is a genuinely unusual pattern — the cheaper half of the lineup has a cheaper home in the higher-GST country — and it exists purely because of a $477 registration-fee difference. [The full New Zealand stack, including its road-user charges](/blog/new-zealand-import-guide/), is worth reading alongside this one.

Thailand takes every row, on the strength of its 0% ACFTA lane and 7% VAT — [with a three-tier excise for imported cars on the horizon](/blog/thailand-import-guide/). The UK is the most expensive of the four, and it is taxed twice before GST-equivalent VAT: 10% duty then 20% VAT, a combination Australia avoids entirely. The UK–Australia gap widens with price — $2,366.80 on the Seagull, $8,760.00 on the Zeekr 001 — because percentage taxes scale where the fixed basket does not. [Why the UK skipped the EU's countervailing duty](/blog/uk-import-guide/) explains why Britain, like Australia, carries no CVD; and if you want the EU benchmark in full, the same Seal's $44,413.48 German landing is unpicked in [our countervailing-duty master table](/blog/china-ev-brand-cvd-master-table/).

Two caveats before anyone books freight. Thailand's headline advantage sits next to a changing excise regime, so verify its current treatment in that guide rather than lifting the number here. And none of these four RHD markets applies a countervailing duty — the tariff wall that dominates EU math simply does not exist anywhere in this comparison.

## The compliance gate: ADR, RAV, NVES — and the RHD filter

Tax is the easy part of an Australian import; compliance is the gate. The sequence runs: satisfy the **Australian Design Rules**, get the vehicle onto the **Register of Approved Vehicles**, then register it. A China-market car carries none of that approval on arrival — our regulatory records describe a framework where ADR conformity is assessed item by item (ADR 79 for emissions and ADR 81/02 for the energy label are two rules explicitly recorded), and the full mapping and latest amendments are UNKNOWN in that dataset. Budget more time and specialist cost here than for Gulf imports, and treat this as the largest non-tax line in the plan.

The **RHD filter** runs before all of it. Australia drives on the left; China's domestic fleet is left-hand drive; so the practical route is the factory RHD export build, which is a subset of each brand's lineup. [Which markets a Chinese EV can actually serve](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) maps the full picture — but for planning purposes, the first question about any model is "is there an RHD export version", not "what does it land at". A landing number for a car that cannot be registered is academic.

Two more policy layers belong on the radar. The **NVES** fleet-average CO2 targets bite at volume: effective 1 January 2025, enforced from 1 July 2025, with penalties recorded around **A$100 per g/km** over target and tradable credits, per our regulatory records — relevant to fleet importers in a way a single-car buyer will never feel. And **ANCAP** star ratings shape buyer expectations across both Australia and New Zealand; our records note the program but leave its founding year unconfirmed, another UNKNOWN to fill locally if it matters to your sales case.

## What buyers should check before committing

1. **Is the exact variant built in RHD export spec?** If not, the process ends here — no landed-cost saving rescues a car that cannot be registered.
2. **What is the ADR compliance and RAV entry path, and who runs it?** This is the largest non-tax cost and the most common source of schedule surprises.
3. **Are you GST-registered?** If so, the 10% GST is typically recoverable and your effective border burden is lower than the headline; if not, it is real money.
4. **Is there LCT exposure?** Our model does not include the Luxury Car Tax and our records do not cover it — confirm treatment before you commit.
5. **What does your broker quote against the $5,350 basket + $500 registration?** Freight and certification are volume-sensitive; the model holds central estimates, not quotes.
6. **If importing at fleet scale, what is your NVES position?** Fleet-average CO2 targets with A$100/g/km penalties change the math for volume, not for single cars.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Australia charge import duty on Chinese EVs?**

No. Our tariff records show a 0.0% standard duty and no countervailing duty; the only border tax in the model is the 10% GST, and it is recoverable for GST-registered businesses. The legal instrument behind the zero rate is not recorded in our dataset — treat that detail as UNKNOWN.

**Is Australia cheaper than New Zealand for a Chinese EV?**

Above roughly $9,540 ex-factory, yes — the two markets cross there. Australia wins by $757.50 on a BYD Seal and by $1,423.00 on a Zeekr 001; New Zealand wins below the crossover, by $30.00 on a BYD Seagull, because its $23 registration fee beats Australia's $500 by more than its five-point-higher GST.

**Can I import a left-hand-drive Chinese EV to Australia?**

Not by the routine path. Australia drives on the left, so a China-market (LHD) car cannot simply be shipped over and registered; the workable route is the manufacturer's RHD export variant. For most of China's domestic lineup, no RHD version exists — check availability before any cost modelling.

**How is the Australian landed cost calculated?**

`landed = base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`, which for Australia reduces to `base × 1.10 + 5,850` (the $5,350 Oceania basket plus a $500 registration fee). The worked Seal example: `24,690 × 1.10 + 5,850 = 33,009.00`. [The full method is documented here](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

**Does the Luxury Car Tax apply to my import?**

UNKNOWN in our records — the LCT does not appear in EV Hub's landed-cost dataset at all, so none of the figures in this article include it. Confirm current treatment and thresholds directly before committing capital.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view, Australia is the strongest large-market landing in our matrix for mid-range and premium Chinese EVs — not because the country taxes consumption lightly (10% is real money), but because it removes the two layers that dominate every other developed market: import duty and countervailing duty. The one thing I would not oversell is ease: "cheapest big-market landing" and "easiest import" are different claims, and compliance plus the RHD filter cut deeper here than the tariff ever did.

### Reasoning

First, the stack is genuinely open — 0% duty, no CVD, 10% GST — and the worked numbers show what that is worth: a $24,690 Seal lands at $33,009.00, the fourth-cheapest of our 30 markets, $5,831.80 below the UK and $11,404.48 below Germany. Only Qatar, Thailand and the UAE land it lower, and all three are smaller or more specialised import lanes. Second, the Oceania comparison is not marketing filler: the crossover at $9,540 is a real structural line — Australia wins above it and the wins widen with price ($757.50 on the Seal, $1,423.00 on the Zeekr 001), because the same $477 registration-fee gap that makes New Zealand the microcar champion gets swamped by a five-point GST difference the moment the base price climbs. Third, the binding constraint is compliance, not cost: ADR/RAV plus RHD availability decide what can even be registered, and two genuine gaps in our records — the legal basis of the zero duty and the LCT treatment — are exactly the kind of questions I would close with a broker before committing capital, precisely because they sit outside the model rather than inside it.

### Disclosure

This is my analysis and opinion, not legal, customs or tax advice, and the landed-cost figures are EV Hub model estimates rather than quotations. The dataset records rates as at 15 September 2026; ADR/RAV paths, the Luxury Car Tax, and the instrument behind the 0% duty should be confirmed with a customs broker, the ATO and the Department of Infrastructure before you commit capital.
