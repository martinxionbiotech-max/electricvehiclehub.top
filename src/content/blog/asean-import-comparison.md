## TL;DR

On the border, **Thailand is the cheapest ASEAN market for a Chinese EV**: 0% import duty under ACFTA plus 7% VAT land a BYD Seal at **$31,068 (+25.8%)**. Malaysia follows at **$33,167** (5% duty plus 10% SST), Singapore at **$37,155** (20% excise duty plus 9% GST), and Indonesia is hardest at **$45,809** (+85.5%, after its CBU exemption expired into a 50% duty). But the border number is only half the story in every one of these markets: Thailand's real constraint is the incoming three-tier excise, Malaysia's is the CBU minimum CIF and power gate, Singapore's is the COE auction — where September 2026 certificates closed at S$133,009 and up — and Indonesia's is the localisation programme. The tariff headline is the least decisive number in all four.

## Key statistics

- **Thailand:** 0% duty (ACFTA entitlement, not a subsidy) + 7% VAT; the 10% excise on imported EVs sits **outside our model**, and the EV Board agreed a three-tier structure on 10 September 2026 with the top tier for fully built-up imports signalled above 10%
- **Malaysia:** 5% duty + 10% sales tax in our model; the full statutory stack for China-origin CBU cars is **5% + 10% excise + 10% sales tax** since 1 January 2026, and since 1 July 2026 CBU imports need a **RM200,000 minimum CIF value and 180 kW minimum power**; CKD EVs keep exemptions until 31 December 2027
- **Singapore:** 20% excise duty + 9% GST; the model **excludes the COE** (September 2026 first exercise: **A S$133,009 · B S$135,001 · E S$137,890**) and the ARF (100% to 320% of OMV)
- **Indonesia:** 50% duty + 11% VAT after the CBU exemption expired on 31 December 2025; the way around it is the 2026–2027 localisation programme (1:1 production commitment, bank guarantee)
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 → **$31,068 Thailand · $33,167 Malaysia · $37,155 Singapore · $45,809 Indonesia**
- **The gaps are exact:** Malaysia−Thailand = **+8.5% of the China base**; Singapore−Malaysia = **+15.3% + $210**; Indonesia−Singapore = **+35.7% − $160** — customs formulas, not estimates
- **Direction of travel:** every one of the four is repricing imports — excise reform (TH), CBU gates and a CKD clock (MY), rebate expiry (SG), localisation deadlines (ID)

## The terms that decide these four markets

- **Landed cost** — the buyer-side cost to bring a China-market car into a market, customs-paid and registered: base price → duty → consumption taxes → VAT/GST → freight → clearance → certification → registration → inland. The full formula is in our [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).
- **ACFTA** — the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area. It is why Chinese EVs enter Thailand at **0%** and Malaysia at **5%**, while Korean- or Japanese-origin cars face their own schedules. The rate needs origin paperwork (Form E), not just a Chinese badge.
- **CBU vs CKD** — completely built-up (a finished import) versus completely knocked-down (kits for local assembly). Thailand and Malaysia are both rewriting their tax codes around this split; it now decides more than the duty line does.
- **Excise duty** — a domestic consumption tax, separate from import duty. Thailand charges 10% on imported EVs (2% for BOI-promoted local production); Malaysia reinstated a 10% excise on CBU EVs in January 2026.
- **SST** — Malaysia's Sales and Service Tax; the sales-tax component on cars is 10%, charged on the duty-paid value.
- **OMV, COE, ARF** — Singapore's three registration concepts: **Open Market Value** (the Customs-assessed value everything keys off), the **Certificate of Entitlement** (a ten-year right to own a car, auctioned twice a month), and the **Additional Registration Fee** (a tiered 100–320% tax on OMV).
- **PPN** — Indonesia's value-added tax, 11% on the duty-paid value.
- **TKDN** — Indonesia's domestic-content requirement; hitting it is the price of staying inside the localisation window.
- **Compounding, not adding** — every stack here multiplies in sequence, so effective loads differ from the sum of the headline rates.

## Four markets, four different gates

The single most useful thing to understand about ASEAN is that no two of these markets gate the same way — and three of the four gates sit **outside the customs formula**:

| Market | The gate that actually decides | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| Thailand | Three-tier excise, agreed 10 Sep 2026; Tier 3 (fully built-up imports) signalled above 10% | Domestic tax, outside our model |
| Malaysia | CBU entry test: ≥ RM200,000 CIF value and ≥ 180 kW power, since 1 July 2026 | Import licensing, outside our model |
| Singapore | COE auction: S$133,009–137,890 in September 2026, plus ARF up to 320% of OMV | Registration, outside our model |
| Indonesia | Localisation: 1:1 production commitment inside the 2026–2027 window | Industrial policy, outside our model |

That table is the honest map of the region. A tariff is a cost you can pay and pass on; a gate is a condition you either satisfy or don't. In three of these four markets, the bigger number is the gate.

## The rate stacks, side by side

| Layer | Thailand | Malaysia | Singapore | Indonesia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **0%** (ACFTA) | **5%** (ACFTA; 30% for non-FTA origins) | **20% excise** on OMV | **50%** |
| Countervailing duty | None | None | None | None |
| Sales / luxury tax | 10% excise on imports — **excluded from model** | 10% excise reinstated Jan 2026 — **excluded from model** | ARF 100–320% of OMV — **excluded** | PPnBM (band unverified — **UNKNOWN**) |
| VAT / GST | **7%** | **10%** SST | **9%** GST | **11%** PPN |
| Registration & plates (our model) | ~$50 | ~$50 | $260 | ~$100 |

None of these four markets applies an EU-style countervailing duty on Chinese EVs — that is an EU measure, not a regional one. The stacks differ entirely in their own design choices.

## Where each market's number stops

**Thailand — the excise layer.** Imported EVs carry a 10% excise that our uniform model excludes; compounded conventionally it adds roughly $2,640 to the Seal, moving the practical floor to about $33,700. The three-tier reform agreed on 10 September 2026 will tax fully built-up imports hardest, with the Excise Department saying it will set the Tier 3 rate first and give importers a grace period. The final rates still need cabinet approval — the specific mechanic, and the statutory base for imported-car excise, remain **UNKNOWN** and should be confirmed with a Thai broker.

**Malaysia — the excise plus the gate.** The catalog model applies 5% duty and 10% sales tax; the statutory stack for China-origin CBU cars since January 2026 is 5% + 10% excise + 10% sales tax. Compounded in the same convention, the Seal's all-in tax-plus-base figure reaches roughly **$36,019 (+45.9%)**, about $2,850 above the modelled number. Separately, from 1 July 2026 a CBU import must carry at least **RM200,000 of CIF value and 180 kW** — a filter that most mid-range models cannot clear as finished cars at all. MITI's own worked example: a car at the RM200,000 CIF floor carries about RM250,000 in cost after the 5/10/10 stack, and arrives at an RRP north of RM300,000 with margins.

**Singapore — the ownership gate.** The border figure excludes the two items that decide Singapore: the COE and the ARF. In the September 2026 first exercise, Category A closed at a record **S$133,009** and Category B at **S$135,001** — roughly US$105,000 for the mainstream certificate, more than six times the entire border cost of a BYD Seagull and nearly three times the Seal's. Power output decides your auction (110 kW or below is Category A; the Seal's 170 kW is Category B). On top, the ARF runs 100% to 320% of OMV, offset for now by VES Band A (up to S$22,500) and EEAI (45% of ARF, capped at S$7,500, expiring 31 December 2026). Budget the certificate before you budget the car.

**Indonesia — the localisation gate.** The 50% duty is the outside-the-programme cost. Inside the 2026–2027 window, producers must manufacture in volumes equivalent to their CBU imports (a 1:1 ratio) against TKDN content requirements, backed by a bank guarantee that is forfeit if they miss. Six automakers enrolled, pledging about 15.5 trillion rupiah (roughly USD 950 million) and capacity of up to 305,000 units a year. Also excluded from our model: PPnBM, the luxury-goods tax, whose current BEV band we could not verify — treat it as **UNKNOWN** and check any quotation for it separately.

## Why every brand is building instead of shipping

The gates explain the factory map, and the factory map explains the medium term. Thailand has built out a full Chinese EV production belt across 2023–2025 — BYD's Rayong plant (150,000 units a year, opened July 2024, with 130,000 Thai deliveries by its second anniversary and exports to the UK, Germany and Belgium), GWM's ex-GM Rayong facility with a Svolt battery line, Neta's Bangkok assembly, Changan's Rayong plant (100,000 a year, planned to reach 200,000), and GAC Aion's Rayong line (50,000, expandable to 100,000). Malaysia's version runs through Geely's 49.9% stake in Proton: the eMAS 7 — a Geely Galaxy E5 by another name — moved from CBU import at RM110,000 to CKD assembly at Tanjong Malim from RM103,800, and Proton took **45.6% of Malaysia's EV market** in the first five months of 2026. Indonesia converted its incentive into exactly this kind of commitment, and is collecting on it through 2027. Singapore is the exception that proves the rule: with no local industry to protect and an auction on ownership, its lever has never been the tariff.

For importers the strategic reading is blunt: in three of these four markets, the tax code now pays you to stop being an importer.

## What buyers should ask before committing

1. **"Which gate applies to my model, and can I pass it?"** — Thai excise class, Malaysian CIF and power floors, Singapore COE strategy, Indonesian localisation plan. The tariff is the easy part.
2. **"Is my ACFTA origin file complete?"** — the 0% Thai rate and the 5% Malaysian rate stand on Form E origin documentation. Models assembled outside China may not qualify; the rate is an origin benefit, not a model benefit.
3. **"Have I priced the excluded layer?"** — add excise in Thailand and Malaysia, the COE and ARF in Singapore, PPnBM in Indonesia. Our border figures are floors by design.
4. **"Am I selling into demand that exists at the new price?"** — Thailand crossed 20% NEV penetration before the excise reform; Chinese brands reached 8.9% of Malaysian registrations in the first five months of 2026; Indonesia's is programme-shaped. The demand is real, but it is priced to the local rules.
5. **"What happens at the deadline?"** — Malaysia's CKD exemption ends 31 December 2027; Indonesia's localisation window runs through 2027; Thailand's Tier 3 rate comes with a grace period; Singapore's EEAI rebate dies on 31 December 2026. In this region, timing is a cost line.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Thailand really the cheapest ASEAN market for a Chinese EV?**
On customs, yes — 0% ACFTA duty and 7% VAT give it the lowest border cost of the four, at +25.8% on a BYD Seal. The caveat is the excise layer: with the current 10% import excise included, the practical floor is nearer $33,700, and the announced three-tier reform is designed to lift it further.

**Does any ASEAN market charge an EU-style countervailing duty on Chinese EVs?**
No. The CVD regime we track is an EU measure under Regulation 2024/2754. Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia apply no anti-subsidy tariff on China-origin EVs — their trade defences run through domestic taxes and licensing instead.

**Why doesn't Singapore top the table if it's the most expensive market?**
Because the table is a border estimate. Singapore's border stack is mid-pack; its ownership system is the world's most expensive. The COE alone — S$133,009 to S$137,890 in the September 2026 first exercise — exceeds the entire border cost of every model we track, and the ARF adds up to 320% of OMV on top.

**Can importers still get Indonesia's old duty-free treatment?**
Only inside the localisation programme: a 1:1 production commitment backed by a bank guarantee, inside the 2026–2027 window, with TKDN content requirements. Outside it, the 50% duty applies — a roughly $13,700 swing on a mid-size sedan versus the expired regime.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view the ASEAN import story has moved off the border entirely, and the four markets now sort by which gate they charge — excise in Thailand, CIF and power floors in Malaysia, the COE auction in Singapore, localisation in Indonesia. If I had to place a bet for each: Thailand for pure customs cost while the excise window is still open, Malaysia only behind a CKD plan or with a compliant premium model, Singapore only with a certificate strategy funded, Indonesia only inside the programme. Shipping finished cars and hoping is the strategy every one of these governments is actively written against.

**Reasoning.** First, the border math is now too small to be decisive: the whole Thailand-versus-Malaysia spread is 8.5 points of base — real, but dwarfed by layers the model excludes, like a six-figure COE or a 10% excise redesign. Second, the direction of travel is uniform: Thailand's Tier 3 rate is signalled above 10%, Malaysia's July 2026 rules superseded a January 2026 regime within six months, Singapore's EEAI rebate expires at the end of this year, and Indonesia's window closes at the end of 2027. Third, the industry has already voted — Thailand's factory belt, Geely's Proton pipeline and Indonesia's pledged capacity show where brands think the taxes will be. Fourth, the demand side supports the pivot: Chinese brands approaching a 9% share in Malaysia, more than 20% NEV penetration in Thailand, programme-driven volumes in Indonesia. The buyer-side conclusion is conditional, not uniform: pay a tariff where you can (Thailand today), pass a content test where you must (Indonesia, Malaysia), and treat Singapore as a certificate business rather than an import business.
