## TL;DR

Indonesia spent three years letting fully assembled electric cars in at **0% import duty** with luxury-tax and VAT relief, on the condition that importers commit to building locally. That scheme expired on **31 December 2025**. Importing a Chinese EV to Indonesia now carries a **50% import duty plus 11% VAT**, and a BYD Seal that would have cleared near **$32,106** under the old regime lands at roughly **$45,809 (+85.5%)**. The threshold is no longer customs — it is whether you sit inside a localisation programme or outside it.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty:** 50% for CBU electric vehicles from 2026, up from a 0% exemption
- **VAT (PPN):** 11%, applied on the duty-paid value
- **Expired incentives:** import-duty exemption plus PPnBM (luxury goods tax) and VAT relief, granted under Ministerial Regulation No. 6/2023 as amended by No. 1/2024
- **Conditions of the old scheme:** bank guarantee plus a 1:1 commitment to produce EVs locally in equivalent volumes
- **Localisation window:** 1 January 2026 – 31 December 2027, with domestic content (TKDN) requirements; failure to comply means forfeiting the guarantee
- **Countervailing duty:** none
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal ($24,690 base) → **$45,809 landed in Indonesia (+85.5%)**
- **The real hurdle:** localisation, not duty

## What actually changed on 1 January 2026

Indonesia's EV incentive was never unconditional. Under the CBU programme, importers got the duty waiver by posting a bank guarantee and committing to build at a 1:1 ratio — one locally produced vehicle for every imported one.

The Ministry of Industry confirmed it would not extend the scheme beyond 2025, and the policy pivoted from promoting imports toward enforcing localisation. Between 1 January 2026 and 31 December 2027, EV producers must manufacture in Indonesia in volumes equivalent to their CBU import quotas, in line with TKDN local-content rules. Companies that miss the target forfeit their bank guarantee to the state.

Six automakers enrolled in the CBU import programme, pledging about 15.5 trillion rupiah (roughly USD 950 million) in investment and combined annual capacity of up to 305,000 units. That is the shape of the deal Indonesia is offering: duty relief as a loan against future industrial capacity, repaid in factories.

## The price of the pivot

Under the exemption, an imported BEV cleared customs with no duty and a reduced tax burden — the conditions that made Indonesia one of the cheapest ASEAN markets for a Chinese EV. Run the same car through the documented formula at the expired 0% duty rate with the standard 11% PPN — `base × (1 + 0) × (1 + 0.11) + fixed costs` — and a BYD Seal lands at about **$32,106**, comparable to Australia and New Zealand.

At the general 50% tariff the same car lands at $45,809. That is a swing of roughly **$13,700 on one vehicle**, driven entirely by policy rather than shipping costs, currency or battery prices. For a distributor, that is not a pricing adjustment; it is a different market.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in Indonesia

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (50% of base) | $12,345 |
| PPN (11% of base + duty) | $4,074 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $4,700 |
| **Total landed** | **$45,809** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Inputs from EV Hub tariff and vehicle records dated 2026-09-15.

This model excludes PPnBM, the luxury-goods tax on certain passenger vehicles. Under the expired incentive, CBU BEVs were relieved of it; under the general regime it can apply. Any quoted Indonesian retail price should be checked for PPnBM separately — we could not verify the current BEV band from a primary source, so we mark it **UNKNOWN** rather than guess.

## The real threshold

Three gates now control an Indonesian import:

1. **Are you inside the localisation programme?** If yes, tariffs are manageable and the real cost is capital expenditure. If no, you pay 50%.
2. **Can you hit TKDN?** Domestic content requirements are progressive, not binary. A partial localisation plan may not clear the bar by the deadline.
3. **Can you fund the guarantee?** The bank guarantee is real money against a 1:1 commitment you must meet within the window.

For a first-time importer with no manufacturing plan, the honest answer is that Indonesia has moved from being one of the most open EV markets in Southeast Asia to one of the most conditional — comparable to Thailand's earlier local-production compacts.

## What buyers should check before committing

- **Is your vehicle in the CBU quota scheme or the general tariff?** The difference is roughly $13,700 on a mid-size sedan.
- **What is your TKDN trajectory?** Model it year by year, not as an end-state percentage.
- **Is PPnBM payable on your model and band?** Confirm from the current regulation rather than from a 2024-era quotation.
- **What happens if you miss the 1:1 target?** The guarantee is forfeit; the carry cost belongs in your business case.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** Indonesia's incentive expiry was not a tax change, it was a market change, and I think the brands that treated it as a pricing problem will exit while the brands that treated it as an investment deadline will own the market.

**Reasoning:** First, a $13,700 swing on a single mid-size car cannot be absorbed by a distributor — it has to be either passed to the buyer or removed by local production, and at the new price point the volume case is much harder. Second, the guarantee structure is unusually well designed: the state collects either a factory or the money, which is why enrolment pulled in roughly USD 950 million of pledges rather than complaints. Third, my concern is the deadline, not the policy. A 1:1 commitment inside a two-year window assumes a supply chain that Indonesia is still building, and the brands most likely to miss are the ones that entered purely to sell cars.

**Disclosure:** This is my editorial read of published regulation and investment reporting, not a legal opinion. Localisation obligations should be confirmed with Indonesian counsel before committing capital.
