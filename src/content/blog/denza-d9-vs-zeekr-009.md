## TL;DR

The [Denza D9](/vehicles/denza-d9/) and the [Zeekr 009](/vehicles/zeekr-009/) are the two cars China builds when the brief says full-size luxury electric MPV — and at the factory gate they sit exactly **$18,200 apart**: $43,630 against $61,830, a 41.7% premium for the Zeekr.

At the EU border they split a second time. Both carry *sampled* countervailing-duty rates below the 20.7% default — **17.0% for the BYD group (Denza), 18.8% for the Geely group (Zeekr)** — and because the cheaper car pays the cheaper rate, the 1.8-point spread widens the landed gap instead of narrowing it. In Germany the D9 lands at **$73,420.65 (+68.3%)**; the 009 at **$102,751.33 (+66.2%)**. That is a **$29,330.68** gap across one customs desk.

Across the seven markets priced below, the gap runs **$19,474 (Thailand) to $29,577 (France)** — and in every one of them the cheaper car, the D9, wears the heavier percentage premium. What the premium buys on the quoted sheets: +120 km of CLTC range, +110 kW, a 1.7-second quicker sprint and a 10-minute charge window. What it does not buy: any relief at the border, where the 009 pays more in duty and VAT in absolute dollars in every EU market.

## What "landed cost" means here

**Landed cost** is the all-in outlay to move a car from the factory gate to a registered, road-legal state in the destination market: ex-factory price, standard import duty, countervailing duty, VAT/GST, and a fixed block covering RoRo freight, customs clearance, certification, registration and inland transport. Like every figure on EV Hub, it compounds rather than adds:

`landed = base × (1+duty) × (1+CVD) × (1+VAT) + fixed costs`

Read the order as carefully as the rates. Duty applies to the base; the countervailing duty applies to the duty-paid value; VAT lands last, on top of both; and the fixed block is flat per vehicle, so it never scales with price. [The methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/) documents the full model, and the [sticker-versus-landed explainer](/blog/sticker-vs-landed-price/) shows why the number a buyer hears is rarely the number the car costs to register. All figures below are USD, computed from the two catalog records; EUR equivalents, where given, use the 23 September 2026 ExchangeRate-API snapshot (1 EUR = $1.1452).

Two scope notes. EU countervailing duties cover battery-electric cars only — which is all this comparison needs, since both records here are BEVs. And unlike the cross-origin duels this catalog usually runs — a Chinese export against a US-built Tesla is a lesson in tariff *architecture*, as [Zeekr 001 vs Model S](/blog/zeekr-001-vs-tesla-model-s/) and [BYD Seal vs Model 3](/blog/byd-seal-vs-tesla-model-3/) both show — this is a same-origin fight: two Chinese groups, two sampled rates, one regulation.

On the sheets, the $18,200 buys a genuinely quicker, longer-legged, faster-charging car.

## Two sampled rates, 1.8 points apart

Both cars pay a producer-specific EU countervailing duty, and both rates are the good kind: individually calculated during the Commission's investigation, not the industry default.

| Rate tier | Who it covers | CVD rate |
|---|---|---|
| Sampled producer | BYD group — includes Denza | 17.0% |
| Sampled producer | Geely group — includes Zeekr | 18.8% |
| Other cooperating companies (non-sampled) | most of the industry | 20.7% |
| Non-cooperating companies | producers outside the process | 35.3% |
| Individually examined | Tesla (Shanghai) | 7.8% |

The rates travel with the group, not the badge: a Denza pays BYD's 17.0% because its exports flow through BYD-group entities, and a Zeekr pays Geely's 18.8% the same way — the arrangement unpacked in [Zeekr's 18.8% explainer](/blog/zeekr-countervailing-duty/) and mapped brand by brand in [the master table](/blog/china-ev-brand-cvd-master-table/). Both sit well below the 20.7% default: against that line, sampling is worth $2,113.14 (D9) and $1,537.78 (009) per car in Germany.

Note the direction of the 1.8-point spread, because it usually runs the other way. On a Geely-versus-BYD pair like the [Galaxy E5 and the Atto 3](/blog/geely-galaxy-e5-vs-byd-atto-3/), the cheaper car pays the *higher* rate, and the differential claws back part of its head start. Here the cheaper car pays the *lower* rate, so the spread compounds into the gap rather than shrinking it. In Germany it is worth **$1,028.01** — the amount the D9 saves against a counterfactual 18.8%, landing at $74,448.66 instead of $73,420.65 — and it costs the 009 **$1,456.83**, which at BYD's 17.0% would land at $101,294.50 instead of $102,751.33. Same 1.8 points, different dollars, because the surcharge compounds off each car's own base: 0.018 × 1.10 × 1.19 ≈ 2.36% of the sticker. Both are counterfactuals under the same regulation, not alternatives you can shop for.

## The gap: $19,474 to $29,577, and the $1,028 inside it

| Market | D9 landed | 009 landed | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | $73,420.65 | $102,751.33 | $29,330.68 |
| France | $73,882.17 | $103,459.33 | $29,577.16 |
| United Kingdom | $63,841.60 | $87,865.60 | $24,024.00 |
| Norway | $61,787.50 | $84,537.50 | $22,750.00 |
| Australia | $53,843.00 | $73,863.00 | $20,020.00 |
| United Arab Emirates | $52,552.08 | $72,617.58 | $20,065.50 |
| Thailand | $51,334.10 | $70,808.10 | $19,474.00 |

Start with the five no-CVD lanes, because there the gap is pure arithmetic — the $18,200 factory gap compounded by duty and VAT, nothing else:

- **United Kingdom:** 18,200 × 1.10 × 1.20 = $24,024.00
- **Norway:** 18,200 × 1.25 = $22,750.00
- **Australia:** 18,200 × 1.10 = $20,020.00
- **United Arab Emirates:** 18,200 × 1.05 × 1.05 = $20,065.50
- **Thailand:** 18,200 × 1.07 = $19,474.00

The two EU rows sit widest because both chains carry duties — and only those rows carry the rate asymmetry. Germany's $29,330.68 decomposes cleanly: **$28,302.67** is the $18,200 gap run through the Geely chain (× 1.10 × 1.188 × 1.19), and **$1,028.01** is the extra the D9 saves because BYD's group rate is the lower one. The two add to $29,330.68.

One presentation warning. The D9 wears the heavier *percentage* premium in all seven markets — +68.3% against +66.2% in Germany, +17.7% against +14.5% in Thailand — even as it wins on dollars everywhere. The reason is mechanical: the same flat block weighs more on a smaller base. At the German rates, $6,600 is 15.1% of the D9's $43,630 against 10.7% of the 009's $61,830. Read dollars for the decision; percentages describe the burden.

## Worked example: both cars through one German customs office

| Line (Germany) | Denza D9 | Zeekr 009 |
|---|---|---|
| Base price (China ex-factory) | $43,630.00 | $61,830.00 |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $4,363.00 | $6,183.00 |
| Countervailing duty (17.0% / 18.8%) | $8,158.81 | $12,786.44 |
| VAT (19%) | $10,668.84 | $15,351.89 |
| RoRo freight | $2,000.00 | $2,000.00 |
| Customs clearance | $350.00 | $350.00 |
| Certification | $3,250.00 | $3,250.00 |
| Registration | $500.00 | $500.00 |
| Inland transport | $500.00 | $500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$73,420.65 (+68.3%)** | **$102,751.33 (+66.2%)** |

Read the tax lines as a single system: the 009 pays $1,820.00 more standard duty, $4,627.63 more countervailing duty and $4,683.05 more VAT than the D9. The $18,200 factory gap grows by **$11,130.68** of duty and VAT to land at **$29,330.68**. That is the compounding formula in one sentence: the border does not split the difference, it multiplies it.

## What the premium buys — and what it doesn't

On the quoted sheets, the 009's $18,200 buys: a 108.0 kWh pack against 103.36 kWh, 740 km CLTC against 620, 310 kW against 200, 440 Nm against 315, 6.9 s to 100 km/h against 8.6, and a 10-minute 10–80% charge window the D9's record does not list. It also quotes 16.8 kWh/100 km efficiency against the D9's 21.9 — while carrying 88 kg more. On paper, this is not badge inflation; it is a different car.

What it does not buy: any relief at the border. In absolute dollars, every market above charges the 009 more than the D9 — more duty and more VAT, or the equivalent GST, in the no-CVD lanes too. The gap never narrows below $19,474, roughly 107% of the factory gap, and reaches $29,577 in France, about 162.5% of it. Whichever market you choose, the premium paid at the factory is a floor for the premium paid at the border, never a ceiling.

Whether that extra capability is worth $19,474–$29,577 landed is a retail-market question, and this model does not answer it — it prices the car to the point of being road-legal, not the price a buyer will pay above it. Warranty provisioning, parts pipeline and resale support, which decide year three of an import program, are outside these numbers for either car.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view this duel is decided at the factory gate, and the customs office merely multiplies the result. The D9's $18,200 head start compounds to a $29,330.68 German gap, and the 1.8-point CVD spread — worth $1,028.01 to the D9 — is a second act, not the plot. If your market can carry a $102,751 landed cost and the retail price that implies, the 009 is the more capable car on the quoted sheets, by a wide margin. If it can't, the D9 is the position the evidence supports, in every one of the seven markets priced here.

### Reasoning

Three numbers do the work. First, price: the gap starts at $18,200 and never shrinks — it lands at $19,474–$29,577, or 107%–162.5% of the factory figure, with the EU rows widest because two duty-carrying chains amplify a gap that a single chain would merely tax. Second, the rate spread: 17.0% against 18.8% looks like noise until it compounds — $1,028.01 and $1,456.83 in Germany depending on which direction you run the counterfactual — and this pair is unusual in that the cheaper car also holds the cheaper rate, so the tariff works with the price gap instead of against it. Third, destination: across the full 30-market records the same two cars swing **$32,974.70** (D9) and **$45,532.70** (009) between their cheapest and dearest cells — a bigger spread than the gap you are deciding about. The spec column is where the premium earns its keep, but it does not move the import math at all.
