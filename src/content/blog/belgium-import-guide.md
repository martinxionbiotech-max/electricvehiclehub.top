## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to Belgium means paying the full EU customs stack: **10% standard duty, a brand-specific countervailing duty of 17% to 35.3%, and 21% VAT** on the running total — the same tariff bill as Germany or the Netherlands, because the EU is one customs territory. On our model a BYD Seal lands at about **$44,699 (+81.0%)**: $250 below the same car into the Netherlands and $286 above Germany, with the difference coming almost entirely from VAT and the registration line, not the tariff. Belgium's real distinctiveness is physical — the cars arrive through Antwerp-Zeebrugge, Europe's largest vehicle port complex, which handled 1,695,000 new vehicles in the first half of 2026 alone.

## Key numbers

- **Import duty:** 10% — the EU's common external tariff on electric passenger cars, identical at every EU border (TARIC heading 8703.80)
- **Countervailing duty (CVD):** brand-specific — 17.0% BYD group · 18.8% Geely, Zeekr, Lynk & Co · 20.7% most other cooperating producers (Neta, Xiaomi, Leapmotor and others) · 35.3% SAIC brands (MG, Maxus, Wuling) · 7.8% Tesla Shanghai
- **VAT:** 21% on the duty-paid value — the same rate as the Netherlands; recoverable for VAT-registered businesses, terminal for private buyers
- **Registration:** ~$150 flat in our records — the lowest line in our Belgium/Germany/France/Netherlands comparison; the regional picture for BEVs is also mild, since Flanders charges them no BIV registration tax at all [Source: European Commission / EAFO]
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 ex-factory → **$44,699 landed (+81.0%)**
- **Gateway volumes:** 1,695,000 new vehicles handled at Antwerp-Zeebrugge in H1 2026, up 7.7% year on year, with vehicle volume from China up 25.5% [Source: Port of Antwerp-Bruges, July 2026]
- **Fleet tilt:** BEVs remain 100% tax-deductible for Belgian companies through 2026 — tapering to 75% by 2031 — while plug-in hybrids lost eligibility in 2024; the private-buyer purchase subsidy is gone [Source: European Commission / EAFO]

## The terms that decide this market

- **Landed cost** — the importer-side cost to bring a China-market car to a Belgian buyer, registered and compliant. Base price → import duty → countervailing duty → VAT → freight → clearance → certification → registration → inland transport. The full formula is documented in our [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).
- **MFN duty** — the EU's "most-favoured-nation" tariff of 10% that applies to cars from any country without a free trade agreement covering them. China has no EU car FTA, so 10% is the floor at every entry point from Antwerp to Zeebrugge to Hamburg.
- **Countervailing duty (CVD)** — an anti-subsidy tariff layered on top of the MFN duty. For Chinese BEVs, the EU set definitive, brand-specific rates in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754, effective 31 October 2024 for five years.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance, freight; the customs value. In our model duty applies to the ex-factory value, with freight, clearance and certification carried as a fixed block per market.
- **Compounding, not adding** — the stack multiplies. Duty comes first, then the CVD is calculated on the duty-paid value, then VAT on the running total. Never sum 10 + 35.3 + 21 = 66.3%; only the line-item math gets it right.
- **One customs territory** — Belgium sits inside the EU customs union, so the duty and CVD bill is identical whether the car clears in Antwerp, Rotterdam or Hamburg. Inside the EU, only VAT, registration fees and local taxes move the number — which is why the Belgium-versus-Netherlands gap in this article is $250, not $2,500.
- **BIV and circulation tax** — Belgium's vehicle taxes are regional. Flanders exempts BEVs from the registration tax (BIV) and from the annual circulation tax; Wallonia replaced its BIV in July 2025 with a formula on power, weight, CO₂ and fuel type, where small BEVs pay very little (a Renault 5 E-Tech at €50; a VW ID.4 at €334); Wallonia and Brussels charge BEVs a minimum circulation tax of €100.98 [Source: European Commission / EAFO].
- **Company-car deductibility** — the benefit that shapes Belgian demand. Battery-electric company cars are 100% deductible through 2026, dropping gradually to 75% by 2031; plug-in hybrids are no longer eligible at all as of 2024, and combustion-vehicle deductibility is being phased out between 2025 and 2028 [Source: European Commission / EAFO].

## What a Chinese EV pays at the Belgian border

| Layer | Rate for a China-origin BEV | Levied on | In our landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty (MFN) | **10%** | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **17% BYD group / 18.8% Geely group / 20.7% default / 35.3% SAIC / 7.8% Tesla Shanghai** | duty-paid value | Yes |
| VAT | **21%** | running total (value + duty + CVD) | Yes |
| Registration & plates | ~$150 flat in our records; the Flemish BIV charge for BEVs is €0 | — | Yes |

Read the table top to bottom and the structure is familiar from every EU market: no national purchase tax on cars beyond VAT (the regional BIV is a registration charge, not a sales tax), but a customs layer that is the heaviest single block in the stack. What is *not* familiar is the registration line. Belgium's ~$150 is the lowest of the four markets we track most closely — Germany's carries $500, France and the Netherlands $400 — and it is the entire reason the same BYD Seal lands $250 cheaper in Belgium than in the Netherlands despite identical 21% VAT rates. Any EU entry point produces the same customs bill, because the EU is one customs territory; the [Germany vs Netherlands vs France comparison](/blog/germany-netherlands-france-import-comparison/) works through how little the VAT spread is worth on its own.

## Landed cost by model (Belgium)

Figures from EV Hub's vehicle records: ex-factory China price, plus the brand's CVD rate, 10% duty, 21% VAT, and the fixed import block (freight, clearance, compliance, registration, inland — $6,250 for Belgium).

| Model | Base (China) | Belgium landed | Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wuling Hongguang MINI EV | $5,000 | $15,254 | +205.1% |
| Neta V | $8,320 | $19,616 | +135.8% |
| MG 4 | $9,690 | $23,700 | +144.6% |
| BYD Dolphin | $14,020 | $28,083 | +100.3% |
| BYD Atto 3 | $16,260 | $31,571 | +94.2% |
| BYD Seal | $24,690 | $44,699 | +81.0% |
| Xiaomi SU7 | $30,320 | $54,960 | +81.3% |
| Zeekr 009 | $61,830 | $104,017 | +68.2% |

Two patterns jump out, and both are structural.

First, **the CVD tier on the badge sets your border bill more than the car's spec sheet does.** The MG 4 and the Neta V start only $1,370 apart ex-factory, yet the MG 4 lands $4,084 higher — because SAIC's 35.3% rate applies to MG while Neta sits at the 20.7% default tier. Put the same price tag on both tiers and the gap is starker still: at an identical $25,000 ex-factory, a 35.3% SAIC car and a 17% BYD car land roughly **$6,000 apart** once duty and VAT compound on the CVD difference. That spread is set at EU level, not by anything a Belgian dealer or importer controls.

Second, **cheap cars wear the fixed block worst.** A $5,000 Wuling pays the same freight, clearance, certification and registration block as a $61,830 Zeekr 009 — about $6,250 in our model — so its percentage premium runs to +205.1% while the Zeekr 009 "only" pays +68.2%. Percentage comparisons between a microcar and a luxury MPV tell you almost nothing about either import decision. Absolute landed cost and incremental tax do.

### Worked example: a BYD Seal in Belgium

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 |
| Import duty (10% of base) | $2,469 |
| Countervailing duty (17% of base + duty) | $4,617 |
| VAT (21% of base + duty + CVD) | $6,673 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $6,250 |
| **Total landed** | **$44,699** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input comes from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 15 September 2026.

One caution on the registration line: the model folds it into a flat $150 for comparability, but Belgium's real cost is region-dependent. Flanders charges a BEV no registration tax and no annual circulation tax; register the same car in Wallonia or Brussels and a small charge applies (a minimum circulation tax of €100.98, plus Wallonia's formula-based BIV where applicable) [Source: European Commission / EAFO]. Treat $44,699 as a floor, not a showroom estimate. The federal registration paperwork specifics could not be verified from primary sources at the time of writing — treat those details as UNKNOWN and confirm with a broker.

## Belgium vs its neighbours: the tariff is identical, the small print is not

This is the comparison the Belgian number is built to make. Belgium, Germany, France and the Netherlands carry the same 10% duty and the same brand CVDs; what differs is VAT and the registration line.

| Market | VAT | Registration line (model) | BYD Seal landed | Gap vs Belgium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 19% | $500 | $44,413 | −$286 |
| France | 20% | $400 | $44,631 | −$68 |
| Belgium | 21% | $150 | $44,699 | — |
| Netherlands | 21% | $400 | $44,949 | +$250 |

Read it as a lesson in what matters. Germany's advantage is purely VAT — two points, worth about $636 on this car — but its registration line is $350 higher, so the net gap is $286. Belgium ties the Netherlands on the 21% headline rate and beats it by $250 on the registration line alone. France sits $68 below Belgium on the mixed effect of one VAT point and a $250 fee difference. No market in the group lands more than 0.7% apart, because **the customs bill — the heavy layer — is set at EU level for all four.** [Source: EU Implementing Regulation 2024/2754; EV Hub records]

For scale, the VAT lever is the one that does move money inside the EU: Sweden's 25% rate puts the same Seal at $45,890. None of it changes at the port, though — the [LHD vs RHD market map](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) matters more than the harbour when you are choosing where a car can ultimately be sold.

## Why Belgium still matters: the gateway where the cars land

Belgium's role in this business is not only as an end market. It is the door. Antwerp-Zeebrugge is the largest vehicle-handling complex in Europe and, by one industry ranking, the third-largest car-terminal cluster in the world, with capacity for more than 3.56 million new cars a year across roughly 974 hectares and storage for about 365,000 vehicles at a time [Source: Marine Insight, September 2025]. That scale shows up in the flow numbers: 1,695,000 new vehicles passed through the port in the first half of 2026 alone, up 7.7% year on year, with growth from China at +25.5% and the port noting China as a continued growth market for vehicle volumes [Source: Port of Antwerp-Bruges, July 2026]. In 2025, China became the leading origin for cars at the port [Source: Port of Antwerp-Bruges, January 2026].

That is also why Belgium ranks so high in trade statistics tracking where Chinese cars are "going". The country is a transshipment artifact as much as a market: Antwerp feeds Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands and Poland, while Zeebrugge serves as a distribution point for Norway, Sweden, Ireland and Finland [Source: Port of Antwerp-Bruges, September 2025]. For an importer, this cuts both ways. Terminal, broker and PDI expertise for China-origin cars exist locally at a depth found nowhere else in Europe; but "import to Belgium" and "sell in Belgium" are different projects, and the port's throughput says more about Europe's appetite than about Belgium's own.

Belgium's own demand, meanwhile, runs through fleets. The tax code treats a battery-electric company car as 100% deductible through 2026 (tapering to 75% by 2031), has already pushed plug-in hybrids out of deductibility entirely, and is phasing out combustion-vehicle deductibility between 2025 and 2028 [Source: European Commission / EAFO]. For a VAT-registered fleet buyer, the 21% import VAT is also recoverable, which pulls the heaviest line in the stack back off the table. Private buyers get neither the deductibility nor the VAT recovery — and the Flemish purchase subsidy that briefly bridged the gap was terminated early on 22 November 2024 when its budget ran out [Source: European Commission / EAFO]. The private-buyer math is the one that stays harsh.

### The tariff regime is not static

The CVD schedule in this article is the regime in force since late 2024 — and it remains the schedule our records apply as of 15 September 2026. The policy space around it is moving, though. Brussels and Beijing have been working on a minimum-price mechanism to resolve the dispute over EV imports: talks resumed in December 2025, the Commission published guidance on the mechanism in January 2026, and in February 2026 the China-built Cupra Tavascan became the first model moved onto the minimum-price track instead of paying the additional duties. The Commission is also weighing extending duties to Chinese-made plug-in hybrids [Source: electrive, December 2025–June 2026]. Whether and when any given Chinese brand moves to such an arrangement cannot be confirmed from public sources — treat brand-level applicability as UNKNOWN and re-check before committing capital.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **Which CVD tier does your brand sit in?** The difference between 17% and 35.3% is worth roughly $6,000 on a $25,000 car once VAT compounds. Confirm the rate for the exact exporting producer, not just the badge.
2. **Are you buying as a business or a private individual?** VAT-registered businesses can deduct the 21% import VAT on commercial use; private buyers cannot. On the Seal above, VAT is $6,673 — the single largest swing available to a buyer.
3. **Plan the registration by region, not by country.** Flanders: zero BIV for BEVs and no annual circulation tax. Wallonia and Brussels: the post-2025 BIV formula and a minimum circulation tax of €100.98 [Source: European Commission / EAFO]. Confirm federal registration paperwork fees with the registration service or a broker — those details could not be verified and are flagged UNKNOWN.
4. **Check the homologation route.** A car with EU whole-vehicle type approval can be registered without extra technical documentation; a China-only homologation needs single-vehicle approval, which is slower and costlier — see our [WVTA vs single-vehicle approval](/blog/wvta-vs-single-vehicle-approval/) explainer.
5. **Left-hand drive and CCS2.** Belgium is a left-hand-drive market on CCS2 charging; a China-market car needs the right charging configuration — our [CCS2 vs GB/T note](/blog/ccs2-vs-gbt-charging/) covers the compatibility cost.
6. **Confirm current customs practice locally.** The exact processing details and fee schedule for a private vehicle import could not be verified from primary sources at the time of writing — treat those specifics as UNKNOWN and confirm with a licensed customs broker before wiring money.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, Belgium is the clearest case in Europe that the customs layer is a bloc-level story and everything else is small print: the same BYD Seal lands within ±0.7% across Belgium, Germany, France and the Netherlands, while the brand-tier spread — BYD's 17% against SAIC's 35.3% — can cost $6,000 more on a single car. Belgium's own edge is not tax arbitrage; it is logistics and fleet structure. The country is where Europe's Chinese cars physically arrive, and its corporate tax code is the most EV-friendly part of its demand story.

**Reasoning.** First, the numbers are not close and not temporary: the duty and CVD multiply a BYD's ex-factory price by 1.287 and a SAIC car's by 1.488 before VAT, and the CVD regulation runs five years from late 2024 — this is the decade's baseline, not a quarter's anomaly. Second, Belgium's policy levers don't offset the customs bill; they redirect it. There is no purchase subsidy left for private buyers (the Flemish scheme collapsed on budget in late 2024), so electrification runs through company-car deductibility, VAT recovery and tax relief — structures that benefit fleets and businesses far more than individuals. Third, the gateway logic is self-reinforcing: 1.7 million vehicles through Antwerp-Zeebrugge in half a year, Chinese volumes growing 25.5%, and deep terminal and broker expertise make Belgium the most operationally practical EU entry point for a China-origin car — even though it gives away almost nothing on price. The one thing I would watch is the minimum-price mechanism; if it spreads beyond first movers, the brand-by-brand math could change faster than the five-year regulation suggests.
