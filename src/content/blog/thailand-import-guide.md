## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to Thailand is one of the cheapest landings in our 30-market data set: **0% import duty under the ASEAN–China free trade area (ACFTA)**, no countervailing duty, and **7% VAT** — a BYD Seal lands at about **$31,068 (+25.8%)**. The catch sits one layer below the tariff: imported EVs already pay a **10% excise tax** that our landed-cost model excludes, and on 10 September 2026 the national EV Board agreed in principle on a **three-tier excise structure that taxes fully imported cars hardest**, with the top rate signalled above 10%. Treat Thailand as a mid-20s-premium import market this month — and a rising one next year.

## Key numbers

- **Import duty:** 0% on China-origin battery-electric vehicles under ACFTA — an FTA entitlement, not a subsidy, and one that Japan- and Korea-origin vehicles do not enjoy under their own FTAs with Thailand
- **Countervailing duty:** none — Thailand applies no EU-style anti-subsidy tariff
- **VAT:** 7% at the border
- **Excise tax:** 10% for imported EVs today (2% for EVs built at Board of Investment (BOI)-promoted Thai plants, 8% for other Thai-built EVs) — not included in the figures below, and the subject of the September 2026 reform
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal, $24,690 ex-factory → **$31,068 landed (+25.8%)**; add the current 10% import excise and the practical floor moves to roughly $33,700
- **Regional check (same Seal):** Malaysia $33,167 (+34.3%), Singapore $37,155 (+50.5%), Indonesia $45,809 (+85.5%)
- **The real threshold:** not the tariff — the excise reform and Thailand's local-production compensation rules

## The terms that decide this market

- **ACFTA** — the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area, in effect since 2005, with preferential concessions now covering roughly 95% of tariff lines. It is the reason a Chinese EV enters Thailand duty-free while a Japanese or Korean one does not.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance and freight; the customs value that the duty and VAT stack is calculated on.
- **CBU vs CKD** — completely built-up (a whole imported car) versus completely knocked-down (kits for local assembly). Thailand's current tax fight is exactly this split.
- **VAT** — value-added tax; Thailand's standard rate is 7%, applied at the border on the import value.
- **Excise tax** — the domestic consumption tax on vehicles, and the only major lever Bangkok can still pull freely (more on why below).
- **Form E / rules of origin** — the ACFTA origin certificate. The zero rate needs proof of origin, satisfied through a change in tariff subheading or 40% regional value content.

## What a Chinese EV actually pays at the Thai border

| Layer | Rate for a China-origin BEV | Levied on | In EV Hub's landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **0%** under ACFTA | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **None** | — | Yes (zero) |
| Excise tax | **10% for imports**; 2% BOI-promoted domestic; 8% other domestic | duty-paid value (verify base) | **No — excluded** |
| VAT | **7%** | import value + duty | Yes |
| Registration & plates | ~$50 flat in our records | — | Yes |

This is why the headline number looks almost too good. Most high-tariff markets we cover stack a double-digit duty with a double-digit consumption tax; Thailand stacks a zero with a seven. The zero is not a policy gift either — it is a treaty commitment. Fulcrum, the ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute's commentary platform, noted in February 2026 that Thailand's zero-percent tariffs on Chinese EVs "were debated more vigorously due to the resulting unequal treatment among market players", because Japan and South Korea do not get the same treatment under their own FTAs with Thailand.

That leaves excise as the pressure valve. Tariffs are locked by the ACFTA; excise is domestic, unilateral, and movable — which is precisely where the September 2026 reform is aimed.

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 2026-09-15. For how the model is built, see [How our landed-cost model works](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).

## Where this model stops: the excise layer

Treat $31,068 as a floor, not a showroom estimate. Our model captures duty and VAT but applies no excise. The current rates, as reported by the Bangkok Post in September 2026, are **2% for EVs built at BOI-promoted Thai plants, 8% for other locally built EVs, and 10% for imported EVs** — and the Excise Department has said the top-tier rate for fully built-up imports "should probably be higher than 10%".

If a 10% excise is applied to the Seal's duty-paid value, it adds about $2,470, which itself enters the 7% VAT base for another ~$170 — roughly $2,640 in total, pushing the practical landed cost to about $33,700 before any reform.

Two things remain UNKNOWN and should be confirmed with a Thai customs broker: the exact statutory base used for imported-car excise (we could not verify the formula from primary sources), and the final three-tier rates, which were undecided when the EV Board agreed the structure in principle.

| Singapore | 20% | 9% | $37,155 | +50.5% |
| Indonesia | 50% | 11% | $45,809 | +85.5% |

The regional picture is why Thailand became the entry market of choice for Chinese brands: it is cheaper to land a car in Bangkok than anywhere else in ASEAN we model. Two footnotes matter before anyone quotes these numbers across borders. Singapore's figure excludes its ARF and COE — which, in practice, dwarf everything above. And Indonesia's cheap chapter is over: its CBU EV exemption expired, and the 50% duty is what Indonesian buyers now face (our Indonesia guide covers that turn in detail). Malaysia's CBU EV tax exemption also ended in 2025, leaving the 5% ACFTA duty plus consumption-tax layers in the stack.

## The factory belt: why the import market built its own replacement

Thailand is no longer just a destination for finished cars. Since 2023, essentially every major Chinese brand selling there has moved production into the country:

- **BYD — Rayong.** Opened 4 July 2024, BYD's first wholly owned overseas passenger-car plant: 150,000 units a year, with the Dolphin, Atto 3, and Seal among the models localized. By the plant's second anniversary in July 2026, BYD's Thai unit had passed 130,000 cumulative deliveries in the market, with 5,000+ jobs, about 93% of them Thai, and local parts procurement at 50%. Exports back to Europe — first Dolphin shipments to the UK, Germany and Belgium — began in August 2025.
- **GWM — Rayong (a former GM plant).** Great Wall acquired the ex-General Motors facility in 2020 and has built Haval models there since 2021. GWM-linked battery maker Svolt Energy rolled its first Thai-made battery pack off a Chonburi line in December 2023, bound first for the Thai-built Ora Good Cat; GWM has also flagged a battery-pack plant investment of up to USD 30 million.
- **Neta — Bangkok.** One of the first Chinese EV assembly plants to fire up in Thailand, starting 30 November 2023: a 20,000-unit line producing the right-hand-drive Neta V-II with local partner Bangchan General Assembly.
- **Changan — Rayong.** Went into operation on 16 May 2025 — first vehicle a right-hand-drive Deepal S05 — on a roughly 10-billion-baht investment; initial capacity 100,000 units a year, planned to grow to 200,000.
- **GAC Aion — Rayong.** Opened 17 July 2024: 50,000 units a year in phase one, expandable to 100,000, building right-hand-drive models including the Aion Y Plus.

The strategic consequence for importers is simple: the brands that once supplied Thailand by ship now build where they sell — and the tax system is being rewritten to reward exactly that.

## The policy swing: from "import to seed" to "produce or pay"

Thailand's EV playbook ran in two phases, and 2026 is the hinge between them.

**Phase one (EV 3.0 / EV 3.5, 2022–2025)** used low import barriers to seed the market: per-car subsidies of THB 50,000–100,000 in 2024 (for models below the THB 2 million price cap) tapering to THB 25,000–50,000 for 2026–27, a reduced 2% excise, and CBU import-duty cuts of up to 40% for 2024–2025 — all conditioned on a promise to start local assembly by 2026 or 2027. Importers under the scheme had to compensate: one locally built car for every two imported if fulfilled by December 2026, one for every three if only by December 2027, with battery localisation to follow from 2026. The carrot worked: new-energy vehicle penetration in Thailand crossed the 20% mark by mid-2024, and Chinese brands supplied most of it.

**Phase two (2026 onward)** flips the incentives toward production. The import-phase duty cuts have expired; subsidies now flow to local assemblers; and on 10 September 2026 the national EV policy committee (the "EV Board") agreed in principle on a **three-tier excise structure**: the lowest rate for EVs meeting local-content requirements or built in Thailand (Tier 1), an intermediate rate for domestic assembly that leans on imported components (Tier 2), and the highest rate for fully built-up imports (Tier 3). The Excise Department has said it will set the Tier 3 rate first and give importers a grace period to adjust; the exact rates still need cabinet approval and were undecided at the time of writing.

Three details in that design tell you where this is going. One, it is deliberately built to push the tax burden onto CBU imports while keeping locally made cars cheap. Two, officials frame it around three goals: domestic investment, a regional export hub role — "particularly for right-hand drive vehicles" — and deeper local supply chains. Three, enforcement of the old scheme's compensation rules is already live: Thai press reported in January 2026 that the Excise Department was tracking at least one importer over outstanding production-compensation units, with penalties possible — we could not access the full report, so treat the specifics as UNKNOWN.

The context is a genuine industrial policy pivot. Thailand is Southeast Asia's largest auto-production hub; the sector accounts for about 3% of GDP and more than half a million jobs, and the country targets BEVs at 30% of vehicle production by 2030 ("30@30"). A surge of low-priced Chinese imports did the adoption job — and then started doing damage to the incumbents the same policy was meant to protect.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **Origin paperwork.** The 0% duty stands on a valid ACFTA Form E; models assembled outside China with Chinese parts may not qualify. The zero is an origin benefit, not a model benefit.
2. **Excise class — and the cabinet decision.** Confirm the current rate class for your model (10% for imports today), then watch the Tier 3 rate when it is published. The signalled grace period is an adjustment window, not a guarantee.
3. **The local-production math.** Subsidies and the lowest excise tier now point at Thai assembly. A pure CBU play should be planned against the top tier.
4. **RHD and homologation.** Thailand is a right-hand-drive market; TISI standards and ATTRIC testing enter the picture under the incentive schemes — vehicles built for left-hand-drive markets do not simply convert.
5. **Regional comparisons.** Thailand currently lands cheaper than Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia on our model — but check Singapore's excluded ARF/COE before quoting cross-border spreadsheets.
6. **Timing.** With Tier 3 rates pending and a grace period signalled, the cost of waiting is a moving target.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, Thailand's zero-duty window for imported Chinese EVs is already closing — not because the 0% ACFTA tariff is about to change, but because Bangkok has decided to tax imports through excise instead, and the September 2026 three-tier decision marks the first explicit step.

**Reasoning.** First, the tariff is a treaty commitment Thailand cannot easily touch — Fulcrum's February 2026 analysis shows the ACFTA upgrade left market access untouched — which is why the pressure has shifted to excise, a domestic lever Bangkok can move alone. Second, the incentive architecture already points one way: subsidies now flow to local assemblers, import-phase duty cuts expired in 2025, and the compensation ratios (1:2 by 2026, 1:3 by 2027) are designed to make pure CBU plays structurally expensive. Third, the market arithmetic still favours buyers today — a +25.8% landing on a BYD Seal is among the cheapest we model — and that is precisely the kind of number a three-tier excise is meant to reprice, with the signalled "higher than 10%" Tier 3 rate pushing landed premiums back toward the 35–40% range.

**Disclosure.** This is my interpretation of published rules, tariff data and press reporting — not legal, tax or customs advice, and not a prediction of the final cabinet decision. Parts of the excise mechanic for imported cars remain unverified (flagged UNKNOWN above); confirm rates and base with a Thai customs broker before committing capital.
