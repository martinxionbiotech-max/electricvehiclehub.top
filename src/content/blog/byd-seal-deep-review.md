## TL;DR

The BYD Seal is a 4,800 mm electric sedan with real export legs, but the number on a Chinese price list is not the number you'll pay in Europe. Bring the Premium RWD into Germany and your landed cost lands at roughly $44,413 — a 79.9% markup over the $24,690 China base price, driven mostly by a 10% import duty, a 17% BYD-specific countervailing duty, and 19% VAT. For an importer, the Seal is a credible Model 3 rival on spec, but the margin question hinges on whether you can hold your retail price above the landed cost once the 17% countervailing duty bites.

When I first sat down to review the BYD Seal for EV Hub, I made a deliberate choice: I'm not going to tell you how it "feels on a twisty B-road." CarExpert, Carwow, Carbuyer and Zecar already do that, and they do it well. What none of them do is answer the question that actually keeps an importer up at night: *if I ship this thing to Europe, what does it actually cost me on the dock, and can I sell it at a profit?*

That's the gap this review fills. If you're a dealer, a distributor, or a fleet buyer weighing a China-sourced Seal against a Tesla Model 3 or a Polestar 2, read on. If you want to know whether the boot fits two golf bags, there are better pages for that.

## What the BYD Seal Actually Is

The Seal is BYD's mid-size electric sedan, part of the company's "Ocean" series. It sits on BYD's dedicated e-Platform 3.0, which is a skateboard-style chassis with the battery integrated into the floor structure. Dimensions: 4,800 mm long, 1,875 mm wide, 1,460 mm tall, with a 2,920 mm wheelbase [Source: BYD official specification sheet].

Why does the wheelbase matter to an importer? Because 2,920 mm puts it in a different tax and classification conversation than, say, a compact hatchback. Some European markets weight vehicle registration and fleet tax by footprint or axle distance. Longer wheelbase, better interior packaging, but a slightly higher tax bill in certain countries.

There are three grades for the 2025 model year, and I'll keep the naming consistent with the European/export catalog rather than the Chinese domestic trims, because the naming is one of the first places an importer gets confused.

### The Three Grades, Flat Out

| Grade | Drivetrain | Battery | Power | 0–100 km/h | WLTP Range |
|-------|-----------|---------|-------|-----------|------------|
| Dynamic | RWD, single motor | 61.4 kWh LFP Blade | 150 kW / 310 Nm | 7.6 s | 460 km |
| Premium | RWD, single motor | 82.6 kWh LFP Blade | 230 kW | 5.9 s | 570 km |
| Performance | AWD, dual motor | 82.6 kWh LFP Blade | 390 kW / 670 Nm | 3.8 s | 520 km |

[Source: BYD official specification sheet; Zecar 2025 BYD Seal review]

Notice something odd? The Performance AWD is the quickest and most powerful, but it has *less* claimed range than the Premium RWD — 520 km against 570 km. That's not a typo. Two motors and an extra 130 kg of kerb weight (2,185 kg vs 2,055 kg) cost you range [Source: BYD Australia spec sheet, kerb weights]. For a fleet buyer doing long highway runs, the Premium RWD is almost always the rational pick. More on that later.

All three use BYD's LFP "Blade" battery. LFP means lithium iron phosphate. It charges slower than NMC chemistry at the very top end, but it tolerates repeated 100% charges without the same degradation anxiety, and in BYD's cell-to-pack layout the pack itself is thin enough to keep the floor low. This matters for export because LFP has no nickel or cobalt in the cathode — which used to be a selling point for duty classification, though the EU's countervailing duty regime has since made that moot anyway.

## Why the Seal Exists (And Why It's Aimed At You)

A quick frame before the numbers. BYD is not some startup shipping cars in a shipping container and hoping for the best. It's the world's largest maker of new-energy vehicles by volume, vertically integrated to a degree that no European or American rival can match — BYD makes its own battery cells, its own semiconductors, and, via its DiLink and DiPilot stacks, its own software [Source: BYD corporate disclosures, 2025].

What that vertical integration means for an importer is simple and often underappreciated: **cost stability and supply certainty.** When a European OEM is hostage to a third-party battery supplier and a semiconductor shortage, BYD is not. The Blade battery is built in-house. The 150 kW DC charging architecture is in-house. The e-Platform 3.0 is in-house. You are less likely to get a phone call telling you your allocation is delayed because a supplier in another country went down.

For a B2B buyer, that's worth real money. A car you can actually get, on schedule, at a predictable invoice, is worth more than a faster car you can't get. This is a point the consumer press rarely makes, because their readers buy one car; your business depends on getting forty of them, forty more the next quarter, and so on.

Now let me take you through the export mechanics that decide whether that supply line works for you.

## The Export Adaptation Question (What Competitors Don't Cover)

Here's the part none of the consumer reviews touch, because their readers don't care. An importer has to care.

### WLTP vs CLTC: The Range You Won't Get

Chinese domestic spec sheets quote range in **CLTC**, which is optimistically high by design. The figures above are WLTP, which is the European standard and far closer to real-world mixed driving. The gap is material.

The Premium is quoted at 570 km WLTP. Its Chinese-market CLTC figure is substantially higher — typically around 650 km rated in NEDC terms on old sheets and north of 600 km in CLTC [Source: BYD Australia spec sheet listing both NEDC and WLTP figures]. If you're building marketing material for a European dealer, you must quote WLTP. If you quote CLTC, a customer will flag the car as under-delivering on range and your credibility is gone. This is a recurring mistake among first-time importers, and it's an expensive one because range claims are the single most scrutinized spec in European EV buying.

### CCS2, Not GB/T

China uses the GB/T charging standard. Europe uses CCS2 (Combo 2) for DC fast charging and Type 2 for AC. The Seal sold through BYD's official European channels comes with CCS2. If you're sourcing a domestic Chinese unit through a grey channel, you may get GB/T and have to retrofit or, in practice, sell it with an adapter that a customer will never trust.

The export-spec Seal fast-charges at up to 150 kW DC. BYD's published figures: 10–80% in 37 minutes, 30–80% in 26 minutes [Source: BYD official charging spec]. That's mid-pack by 2025 standards — a Model 3 Long Range and most 800-volt Korean cars beat it on peak rate. But 150 kW with LFP is honest, and LFP holds a flatter charge curve so the real-world 30–80% time is closer to the published claim than you'd get from an NMC pack that only hits its peak for two minutes.

### LHD vs RHD

Left-hand drive is the default for continental Europe; right-hand drive matters for the UK, Ireland, Malta and Cyprus. BYD produces the Seal in both, but the RHD allocation has historically lagged LHD supply. If your market is mainland Europe, this is a non-issue. If you're exporting to the UK post-Brexit, you also inherit a different certification and VAT treatment, which I flag but don't price out here — this review's cost model is Germany-specific.

### WLTP Certification and Homologation: The Quiet Killers

Of all the export pitfalls, homologation is the one that surprises people, because it's not a line item on the seller's invoice. To register a Seal in the EU, the car needs either **full EU type approval** (the same path the official importer uses) or **single-vehicle / national individual approval** if you're importing in small batches [Source: EU type-approval framework, Regulation (EU) 2018/858].

The full type-approval route is expensive — tens of thousands of euros of testing if you're starting from scratch — but BYD already holds EU type approval for the Seal through its official channel, so a legitimate importer sourcing through BYD-approved channels can often ride that approval rather than paying to establish it. This is a huge practical advantage over a genuinely grey-market car from a brand that never entered Europe.

The individual-approval route is cheaper per car but caps your volume and, in some member states, comes with restrictions — for example, Germany's §21 StVZO single-vehicle approval can limit resale flexibility and may require a full technical inspection. I've priced $3,250 into the landed-cost table as a blended certification figure for small-to-medium batch imports [Source: EV Hub landed-cost methodology], but if you're doing serious volume you should budget for the type-approval amortization path instead, which spreads a larger fixed cost over a bigger run.

### Warranty and Service: What You're Actually Selling

A car without a service story is a hard sell in Germany, where the TÜV inspection culture and the warranty expectation are both baked in. BYD's official European warranty covers the battery for up to 8 years / 160,000 km on the LFP Blade pack and the vehicle for up to 6 years / 150,000 km, depending on market [Source: BYD Europe warranty terms].

Here's the catch for an independent importer: if you source outside BYD's official channel, the manufacturer warranty may not transfer cleanly to your customer, which is why several independent European importers of Chinese EVs now write their own importer warranty on top [Source: Auto-China importer practice, 2025]. You need to plan for that. A thin-margin model falls apart the moment you're provisioning for battery claims you didn't price in.

Also worth noting: the Seal uses an 82.6 kWh LFP pack specifically because LFP's chemistry is gentler, which lowers the probability of the kind of high-cost warranty event that NMC packs occasionally produce. That doesn't eliminate your warranty provisioning — but it does shift the risk curve in your favor [Source: BYD Blade Battery chemistry documentation].

## The Engineering Under the Skin (Why the Platform Matters)

Consumer reviews skip this because it's invisible. An importer shouldn't, because it's where the durable value lives and where the cost was engineered out.

The Seal rides on e-Platform 3.0, BYD's dedicated EV architecture. The defining feature is **CTB — cell-to-body** — which integrates the Blade battery pack directly into the body structure rather than bolting a separate pack under a floor pan [Source: BYD e-Platform 3.0 technical disclosure].

The practical consequences are threefold, and all three matter to a buyer:

1. **Stiffer body.** Using the pack as a stressed structural member raises torsional rigidity, which is why the Seal's ride is so noticeably composed — the chassis doesn't flex and wobble like early budget EVs [Source: CarExpert dynamics assessment of the Seal].
2. **Lower floor.** Because the cells sit inside the body rather than in a thick external housing, the floor height drops, which improves packaging and headroom in a 1,460 mm-tall sedan.
3. **Lighter for the battery size.** The 82.6 kWh Premium weighs 2,055 kg — heavy by ICE standards, but competitive for a 570 km WLTP EV of this size [Source: BYD spec sheet kerb weight].

The drag coefficient is 0.219 Cd, which is genuinely good and is a major reason the Premium extracts 570 km from 82.6 kWh rather than the 500-odd km a less-slippery shape would return [Source: BYD official aerodynamic data]. For a fleet buyer doing autobahn speeds, Cd matters more than power — drag, not weight, dominates highway energy consumption.

### The Battery, De-Mystified

LFP (lithium iron phosphate) gets a bad rap from people who remember early LFP EVs being slow and range-poor. That's outdated. The Seal's LFP Blade delivers competitive energy density in a thin pack and, crucially, **doesn't mind being charged to 100% routinely** the way NMC degrades faster when held at full. BYD's Blade battery documentation and independent testing both support the longevity claim [Source: BYD Blade Battery documentation; independent LFP vs NMC cycle-life studies].

The trade-off is real, though: LFP generally peaks at a lower charge rate than the best NMC, which is why the Seal tops out at 150 kW DC while an 800-volt NMC car will hit 250 kW or more. If your customers are road-trippers who live by the fast-charging stopwatch, this is a genuine weakness. If they're daily commuters charging at home overnight, it's nearly irrelevant. Know your customer.

## The Landed Cost Breakdown (EV Hub Core Data)

This is where we earn our keep versus everyone else. Carwow will tell you the Seal "starts at £45,730." CarExpert gives you an Australian drive-away figure. Neither tells you what happens between the Shenzhen factory gate and a German forecourt.

Before I show the table, one framing point: the order of operations matters for the math. You don't add VAT last on a single lump sum — you add the import duty to the vehicle value first, then the countervailing duty, and *then* VAT on top of that combined customs value. Compounding the duties before VAT is exactly why the percentage keeps climbing past what people expect. A 10% duty plus a 17% duty does not equal 27%; because the VAT is then levied on the duty-inclusive value, the effective total markup lands at ~80% rather than the ~54% a naive add-up would suggest.

Here is the full chain for a **Premium RWD**, using the China base price of $24,690 as the starting point [Source: EV Hub landed-cost record, BYD China MSRP].

| Cost Line Item | Amount (USD) | Notes |
|----------------|-------------|-------|
| Base price (China) | $24,690 | Premium grade, ex-factory |
| Standard import duty (10%) | $2,469 | Applied to vehicle value [Source: EU Common Customs Tariff] |
| Countervailing duty (BYD, 17%) | $4,617 | BYD-specific anti-subsidy rate [Source: European Commission] |
| VAT (19%) | $6,037 | German standard rate, on value + duties [Source: German tax code] |
| RoRo freight | $2,000 | Roll-on/roll-off, Shenzhen → Bremerhaven |
| Customs clearance | $350 | Broker + handling |
| Certification | $3,250 | EU type approval / individual approval |
| Registration | $500 | First registration, Germany |
| Inland transport | $500 | Port → dealer lot |
| **Total landed** | **$44,413** | **+79.9% over base price** |

[Source: EV Hub landed-cost methodology]

That +79.9% is the number I want every importer to internalize before they even look at a spec sheet. You are not buying a $24,690 car. You are buying a $44,413 car, and that's before any dealer margin, before warranty provisioning, before currency risk on the USD/CNY and EUR/USD legs, and before whatever local market incentives or EV purchase subsidies you can or can't stack.

### Why the Duties Stack the Way They Do

The EU hit Chinese-built EVs with two layers. First, the standard 10% Most-Favored-Nation import duty that any non-EU car pays [Source: EU Common Customs Tariff]. Second, a set of **countervailing duties** — anti-subsidy tariffs — in differ by manufacturer because they're pegged to how much state subsidy the Commission determined each company received in its investigation.

The rates: **BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%** [Source: European Commission countervailing duty regulation]. Tesla, which builds Model 3s in Shanghai, got a lower individual rate because it cooperated and demonstrated less subsidy exposure — which is exactly why a China-built Model 3 can still undercut a Seal in some EU markets despite the Seal's cheaper ex-factory price.

This is the single most important strategic fact for an importer choosing between a Seal and a Model 3. The car is not the product; the *duty rate attached to the manufacturer* is a big part of the product. And those rates are not static — the Commission revisits them, and BYD's announced moves toward European assembly (Hungary, and reportedly a second plant) are squarely aimed at getting out from under the 17%.

### What You Can Recover

Two lines in that table are worth a second look. **VAT** is recoverable if you're VAT-registered in the destination market — you claim it back on the input side and charge it forward to your buyer, so it's a cash-flow item, not a margin cost, for a proper B2B operation. **The countervailing duty is not recoverable.** It's a hard cost, and it's the single largest non-VAT line after the base price at $4,617.

The certification line ($3,250) covers EU type approval or the cheaper single-vehicle/individual approval route if you're bringing in small batches. Batch your imports to amortize it, or you'll leed margin on low volume.

## BYD Seal vs Tesla Model 3: The Table That Matters

Most importers aren't deciding "Seal or nothing." They're deciding "Seal or Model 3." Here's the honest side-by-side on the points that drive a purchase decision, not just the headline –100 number.

| Criterion | BYD Seal Premium RWD | Tesla Model 3 Long Range RWD |
|----------|---------------------|------------------------------|
| Battery / chemistry | 82.6 kWh LFP Blade | ~78 kWh (varies) NMC/LFP mix |
| WLTP range | 570 km | ~700 km (varies by market) |
| Power | 230 kW | ~208 kW |
| 0–100 km/h | 5.9 s | ~5.9 s |
| Max DC charge | 150 kW | 250 kW |
| Drawn-from-China duty | 17% countervailing (BYD) | lower individual rate (Tesla) |
| Charge network | n/a (CCS2 universal) | Supercharger network |
| Interior | physical controls + 15.6" rotating screen | minimal, screen-centric |

[Source: BYD official spec; Tesla Model 3 spec; European Commission duty rates]

The Model 3 wins on peak charge rate, range claims, and the Supercharger network. The Seal wins on interior tactility (physical buttons plus a rotating central display, which more conservative European buyers actually prefer), ride compliance, and — in raw terms — ex-factory price.

But look at the duty row. That's the whole ballgame right now. Tesla's lower individual countervailing rate means its China-built Model 3 lands cheaper relative to its spec than a Seal does. BYD knows this, which is why the Seal's value proposition in Europe leans harder on dealer-channel support and warranty than on pure price. If you're an importer, don't assume the cheaper China car is the better import. Run the landed-cost math line by line before you assume.

## The Bigger Picture: BYD's European Bet and What It Means for You

It's worth zooming out, because your import decision is happening against a backdrop that will shift underneath you.

BYD is not treating Europe as an afterthought. It is, reportedly, building a passenger-car plant in Hungary and has signalled a second European assembly site [Source: BYD Europe manufacturing announcements, 2024–2025]. The strategic logic is transparent: assemble inside the EU and you sidestep the 10% import duty and the 17% countervailing duty entirely, because the car is no longer an import.

For an independent importer reading this today, that's a double-edged sword. On one hand, it validates the product and the demand — BYD wouldn't commit billions to Europe if the Seal and its siblings weren't selling. On the other, it means the window for importing China-built units at today's economics has a finite life. When the Hungarian plant comes online, the cost base of an EU-assembled Seal will reset *below* what you can achieve importing from China, and BYD's own channel will own that advantage.

So the realistic importer thesis is: **there is a medium-term opportunity, but it is time-boxed.** The importers who win are the ones who build dealer relationships, service capability, and brand goodwill *now*, while the China-import path is still the fastest way to get volume into the market. If you're waiting to be sure, you'll be late.

### The Residual Value Problem (And Why It's Actually a Feature for Some)

New brands in a new market have roughly zero used-car price history. That scares conventional finance — and it should. But here's the counterintuitive bit: LFP batteries' slower degradation is quietly becoming the foundation of a used-EV value story, because a 5-year-old LFP car with 80% of its range intact is a more rational secondhand buy than a 5-year-old NMC car of unknown charge-cycle abuse [Source: LFP vs NMC cycle-life studies; BYD Blade Battery documentation].

If you're a dealer holding trade-ins, this matters. The Seal is one of the few EVs where I'd be relatively comfortable underwriting a residual based on battery chemistry rather than brand cachet. That's a genuine, if early-stage, edge — and it's the kind of thing the consumer press never connects to an import decision.

## Who Should Import the Seal — and Who Shouldn't

Let me be blunt, because too many B2B guides dance around this. The Seal is not for every importer.

**You should be looking hard at the Seal if:**

• You already have (or can build) a sales and service network in a mainland European market, because a car without service support is a one-sale product, not a business [Source: EV Hub importer practice].
• Your thesis is strategic positioning in Chinese EVs, not a single-model margin play — you want to be the go-to dealer when BYD's European plants reset the cost base.
• Your customers care about interior quality, ride comfort, and total cost of ownership over peak charging speed and software polish.
• You are VAT-registered in the destination market and can recover the 19% input VAT rather than eating it as margin [Source: German VAT recovery rules for B2B importers].

**You should probably steer clear if:**

• You're running a pure thin-margin, high-volume grey-import model with no service or warranty story. The 17% countervailing duty will eat you alive [Source: European Commission duty rate].
• Your buyers are fast-charging speed freaks who will cross-shop the 150 kW Seal against 250 kW rivals and walk away.
• You're arbitraging price alone. The landed cost is high enough that there is no easy price arbitrage left — the margin is in positioning and service, not in the invoice delta.

None of this is a value judgment on the car itself. It's a matching exercise between the product's economics and your business model. The Seal is a good car; it is not a magic-margin car.

## What We Found Reviewing the Car

This is the part where I put the editor hat on and tell you what actually stuck out from hands-on evaluation and the independent road tests, because a B2B buyer still needs to know what they're selling.

### Strengths

✅ **Interior quality above its price band.** Multiple UK and Australian testers noted the cabin materials punch above the Seal's price — "genuinely premium" was the recurring phrase [Source: CarExpert 2025 Seal review]. For a dealer, this is the easiest thing to sell in a showroom: it looks and feels more expensive than the invoice.

✅ **Ride and refinement.** The Seal rides more compliantly than a Model 3, which appeals to the European buyer who found the Tesla too firm over broken tarmac [Source: Carwow BYD Seal review].

✅ **LFP longevity story.** An 82.6 kWh LFP pack that tolerates routine 100% charges gives you a warranty-claims-adjacent sales pitch: the battery is the least likely part of the car to be a service headache [Source: BYD Blade Battery documentation].

✅ **The Premium RWD is the value sweet spot.** 570 km WLTP from 230 kW at a lower kerb weight than the AWD is the rational fleet and retail pick [Source: BYD spec sheet].

### Weaknesses

❌ **Charging is mid-pack.** 150 kW peak, 26 minutes for 30–80%, is fine but not class-leading, and Europe's fast-charging rollout means buyers are increasingly rate-sensitive [Source: BYD charging spec].

❌ **The countervailing duty erodes the price advantage.** The 17% rate plus 10% standard duty narrows the gap against a China-built Model 3, which pays a lower individual rate [Source: European Commission duty rates].

❌ **Residual-value uncertainty.** BYD is new in Europe, and clean used-EV pricing for Seal is still thin. A dealer taking a trade-in is pricing into the unknown [Source: EV Hub market observation, mid-2025].

❌ **Software is not Tesla.** The infotainment and driver-assist stack is competent but not class-reference. Buyers cross-shopping a Model 3 will feel the difference in software polish [Source: Carwow review; multiple testers].

## The Importer's Margin Question, Worked Through

Let me put a concrete number on the anxiety. Take the Premium RWD again.

- Landed cost in Germany: **$44,413** [Source: EV Hub landed-cost breakdown]
- A realistic German dealer list price for a comparable 2025 Seal Premium, before any national EV bonus, sits around €45,000–€48,000 depending on spec and market timing [Source: Reddit r/BYD owner reports, Germany market prices ≈ €38,000–€41,000 for Comfort trim, higher for top grades].

Convert that back to USD and you're looking at roughly $48,000–$51,000 at a typical mid-2025 USD/EUR rate. That leaves a gross spread of maybe $4,000–$7,000 between landed cost and list — and out of that comes your dealer margin, warranty provisioning, floorplan interest, and any discounting needed to move metal given BYD's own official channel and the Tesla price pressure.

The upshot: **this is not a high-margin import right now.** It's a market-entry bet. You import a Seal not to make rich margin on unit one, but to establish a brand presence, build service capability, and position yourself for when the EU duty regime softens or BYD's European plants come online and reset the cost base.

If your model is thin-margin and high-volume, look elsewhere. If your model is strategic positioning with a credible product, the Seal earns its place — but only if you nail the landed-cost math first.

## FAQ

**Q: Is the WLTP figure real, or should I expect less in practice?**
A: WLTP is the honest European standard, unlike CLTC. Expect real-world mixed driving to land 10–15% below the WLTP figure in cold weather, roughly in line with most EVs. The Performance AWD tested at 18.6 kWh/100 km in independent testing, which implies around 440–450 km of real range from its 82.6 kWh pack in mixed conditions [Source: CarExpert road test efficiency figure].

**Q: How much does it actually cost to land a BYD Seal in Germany?**
A: For the Premium RWD, about $44,413 all-in, a +79.9% markup over the $24,690 China base price, driven by 10% duty, 17% BYD countervailing duty, 19% VAT, freight and certification [Source: EV Hub landed-cost methodology]. Use our calculator to model your own spec and market.

**Q: Does the Seal need CCS2 or GB/T charging?**
A: For Europe you need CCS2 (DC) and Type 2 (AC). Export-spec Seals come with CCS2. Sourcing a domestic Chinese unit will leave you with GB/T and a retrofit or adapter problem you don't want to explain to a customer.

**Q: Which grade should an importer stock?**
A: The Premium RWD in almost every case. It has the longest range (570 km), the lowest kerb weight of the 82.6 kWh grades, and avoids the AWD's range penalty while still hitting 5.9s to 100 km/h [Source: BYD spec sheet].

**Q: Is the Seal cheaper to import than a Model 3?**
A: Not necessarily. The Seal's ex-factory price is lower, but BYD's 17% countervailing duty is higher than Tesla's individual rate, so the landed differential is smaller than the sticker differential suggests [Source: European Commission duty rates]. Run both through the landed-cost calculator before deciding.

## Key Takeaways

1. **A $24,690 China price becomes a $44,413 German landed cost** — a +79.9% markup — and the 17% BYD countervailing duty is the biggest single hard cost after VAT [Source: EV Hub landed-cost breakdown].
2. **The duty regime is manufacturer-specific, not car-specific.** BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3% — a Tesla built in the same Shanghai region pays less, which changes the import calculus entirely [Source: European Commission countervailing duty regulation].
3. **Quote WLTP, never CLTC, and spec CCS2.** These two export-adaptation errors are the fastest way to burn dealer credibility in Europe [Source: BYD official spec; charging-standard mapping].
4. **Stock the Premium RWD.** 570 km WLTP, lowest kerb weight, 5.9s, and the best range-to-price ratio of the three grades [Source: BYD spec sheet].
5. **This is a strategic, not a high-margin, import.** The gross spread between landed cost and German list price is thin (roughly $4,000–$7,000), so treat the Seal as a market-entry play, not a cash cow [Source: EV Hub landed-cost + market price analysis].

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Our read on the Seal: a good car inside a time-limited trade. The defensible thesis is strategic positioning, not per-unit margin — and the variable that decides which you get is the duty rate attached to the manufacturer, not the spec sheet.

**Reasoning.** First, the economics are settled once compounded: $24,690 becomes $44,413 because the 10% duty, 17% countervailing duty and 19% VAT multiply rather than add — the duty is non-recoverable, the VAT is not. Second, the gross spread between landed cost and German list is only about $4,000–$7,000 — too thin to absorb dealer margin, warranty provisioning and discounting at once; it is a market-entry budget, not a profit line. Third, the cost base is about to move: BYD's European assembly plan aims to escape the 17% duty entirely, resetting the math in its own channel's favor when it lands. Fourth, the product case holds where the data says it holds: the Premium RWD's 570 km WLTP and lower kerb weight make it the rational grade, and LFP's cycle life supports a residual-value story the consumer press never connects to an import decision.
