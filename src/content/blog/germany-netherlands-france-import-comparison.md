## TL;DR

If you're choosing between importing a Chinese EV into Germany, France, or the Netherlands, the honest answer is: **the landed-cost difference is small enough to ignore — roughly 1% of the landed cost — and Germany is only marginally cheapest because it has the lowest VAT.**

Here's the thing most buyers don't realize until they run the numbers: **the EU is a customs union.** The 10% standard duty and the brand-specific countervailing duty (17% for BYD, 18.8% for Geely, 35.3% for SAIC) are identical no matter which EU country the car enters. The *only* variable that changes between Germany, France, and the Netherlands is the VAT rate — 19%, 20%, and 21% respectively.

On a $24,690 BYD Seal, that two-point VAT spread is worth about **$536** — $44,413 in Germany versus $44,949 in the Netherlands. On a $61,830 Zeekr 009 it's about **$1,516**. Not nothing, but a rounding error compared to what actually decides your import economics: homologation, registration, incentives, and where your buyer lives.

## Key statistics

- **EU standard import duty:** 10% — identical in Germany, France, and the Netherlands
- **Countervailing duty:** brand-specific (BYD 17% / Geely 18.8% / SAIC 35.3%) — identical across the EU
- **VAT:** Germany 19% · France 20% · Netherlands 21% — the *only* variable between the three
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal → $44,413 (DE) vs $44,631 (FR) vs $44,949 (NL) — a $536 spread
- **Bottom line:** the VAT-driven difference is ~1% of landed cost, and is rarely the deciding factor

## Why the tariff stack is identical across the EU

This is the single most misunderstood point in the whole import conversation. Because the EU is a **single customs territory**, the duties on a Chinese EV are set at the bloc level and collected identically at every EU border:

- **Standard duty (10%)** — the EU's common external tariff, the same whether the car clears customs in Hamburg, Le Havre, or Rotterdam.
- **Countervailing duty (17–35.3%)** — set by producer in [Regulation 2024/2754](/blog/byd-countervailing-duty-landed-cost/), not by destination country. A BYD pays 17% at *any* EU entry point.

So the "cheapest EU country to import into" question collapses almost entirely to one thing: **the VAT rate**. Germany charges 19%, France 20%, and the Netherlands 21%. That's the entire difference in our landed-cost data.

## The real number: same car, three EU markets

Here are seven models spanning the full price range, landed in all three markets (duty and CVD identical, only VAT differs):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wuling MINI EV | $5,000 | $15,455 | $15,430 | $15,504 | $74 |
| Seagull | $8,940 | $20,292 | $20,307 | $20,422 | $130 |
| MG4 | $9,690 | $23,762 | $23,806 | $23,950 | $188 |
| Dolphin | $14,020 | $28,072 | $28,152 | $28,333 | $261 |
| Zeekr X | $21,940 | $40,719 | $40,905 | $41,192 | $473 |
| BYD Seal | $24,690 | $44,413 | $44,631 | $44,949 | $536 |
| Zeekr 009 | $61,830 | $102,751 | $103,459 | $104,267 | $1,516 |

The pattern is unambiguous: **Germany is cheapest, France is in the middle, the Netherlands is most expensive — and the gap is tiny.** The spread scales with price because VAT is a percentage, but even on a six-figure Zeekr 009 it's only $1,516 — about 1.5% of the landed cost.

A note on methodology: our landed-cost model applies the same fixed import costs (freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland) across all three markets. In reality these vary slightly — Rotterdam versus Hamburg versus Le Havre freight, country-specific registration fees — but those variations are also modest relative to the factors below.

## Where the math tilts

Take the article's headline car, the $24,690 BYD Seal. Duty and the 17% BYD countervailing duty lift the VAT base to $24,690 × 1.10 × 1.17 = $31,776. Charging each country's VAT rate on that same base:

| Country | VAT rate | VAT on a $31,776 base | Difference vs Germany |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 19% | $6,037 | — |
| France | 20% | $6,355 | +$318 |
| Netherlands | 21% | $6,673 | +$636 |

One VAT point is worth $318 on this car; the full German–Dutch gap is $636. Then the fixed side: the registration line in the cost stack runs $500 in Germany against $400 in France and the Netherlands — a $100 rebate on the same car. Net of that fee, France lands +$218 and the Netherlands +$536 above Germany, which is exactly the $218 and $536 spreads in the table above, once the tax leg and the fee leg are separated.

Which of the three is friendliest to a small-batch import? On fees alone, France and the Netherlands, by $100 per car — and at the cheap end that edge can flip the ranking outright: on the $5,000 Wuling MINI EV, France lands about $25 below Germany ($15,430 against $15,455), because one VAT point there is $74 against the $100 fee saving. Above roughly a $10,000 VAT base, the one-point VAT outweighs the fee and Germany's ordering holds.

## What actually decides your EU market (and it's not VAT)

If VAT differences are ~1%, what should actually drive your choice of entry country? Four things that dwarf the $536 VAT spread on a Seal:

1. **Homologation route.** Every country accepts EU type-approval, but for a Chinese-market car that *wasn't* built to EU type-approval, you need individual vehicle approval (IVA) or national single-vehicle approval — and the cost, timeline, and paperwork differ by country. The Netherlands has a relatively streamlined national route; Germany's TÜV-based path is rigorous but well-documented; France's reception is paperwork-heavy. This can cost thousands and swing far more than VAT.

2. **EV incentives.** Purchase subsidies and tax breaks are national. The Netherlands and France have historically offered purchase grants and BIK (benefit-in-kind) advantages that Germany has scaled back. A grant of €2,000–€4,000 completely erases the VAT difference.

3. **Where your buyer actually lives.** This is the boring but decisive point: you import into the country where the car will be registered. You cannot "import cheaply into Germany" and then register in France without paying French VAT and re-doing paperwork. The entry country and the registration country are, in practice, the same decision.

4. **Registration tax and recurring costs.** Registration tax (bpm in the Netherlands, the malus/weight component in France) and annual road tax differ materially and are usually far larger than the VAT spread.

## The honest recommendation

Treat "which EU country is cheapest" as the wrong question. The right questions are: *where is my buyer, what's the homologation path there, and are there incentives that offset the import costs?* The 1% VAT difference between Germany, France, and the Netherlands is real but is almost never the thing that decides whether an import makes sense.

## What buyers should ask before picking an EU entry country

1. **"Where will the car actually be registered?"** — the entry country and registration country are effectively the same decision. Don't plan to "save VAT" by entering in a different country than the buyer.
2. **"What's the homologation route and cost in that country?"** — for non-type-approved Chinese cars, individual approval costs and timelines vary by country and can exceed the entire VAT spread.
3. **"Are there EV incentives I'm eligible for?"** — a national purchase grant or tax break will outweigh the 1–2 point VAT difference every time.
4. **"What are the registration and annual road taxes?"** — these often exceed the import-duty and VAT differences combined.
5. **"Am I comparing the same CVD rate?"** — the countervailing duty is brand-specific (17–35.3%), not country-specific. Don't confuse a country's VAT with the brand's CVD.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Germany actually cheaper to import into than France or the Netherlands?**
Marginally, yes — because Germany's VAT is 19% versus 20% and 21%. But the difference is about 1% of landed cost ($536 on a BYD Seal), which is smaller than real-world variation in homologation and registration.

**Does the countervailing duty differ between EU countries?**
No. It's set at the EU level by producer (BYD 17%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%) and is identical at every EU entry point. The only country-level variable is VAT.

**Can I import into a low-VAT country and register elsewhere in the EU?**
No, not meaningfully. You pay the VAT of the country of final registration. Trying to arbitrage entry-country VAT just creates duplicate paperwork; the car's final registration country determines the VAT you owe.

**Why is the Netherlands the most expensive?**
Purely its 21% VAT — the highest standard rate among the three. Its actual landed-cost premium over Germany is about 1–1.5%.


## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view "which EU country is cheapest to import into" is a question that costs more to ask than it saves: the customs stack is identical across the bloc, and the VAT spread it turns on is roughly 1% of landed cost.

**Reasoning.** First, the duties are bloc-level: a BYD pays 17% and a SAIC pays 35.3% whether the car clears in Hamburg, Le Havre, or Rotterdam, so the country-level variable is only VAT (19% vs 20% vs 21%). Second, the resulting spreads are roundings next to everything else: $536 between Germany and the Netherlands on a $24,690 Seal, and $1,516 even on a $61,830 Zeekr 009 — about a point and a half. Third, the factors that actually swing an import are national and non-tariff: a single €2,000–€4,000 purchase grant erases the VAT difference several times over, and homologation routes diverge far more than rates do.
