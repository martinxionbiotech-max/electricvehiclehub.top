## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to New Zealand costs **0% import duty plus 15% GST**, with no countervailing duty — a BYD Seal at $24,690 ex-factory lands at about **$33,766.5 (+36.8%)**. Two things decide whether the market works for you: since **1 April 2024** light EVs pay **road user charges of NZ$76 per 1,000 km**, and New Zealand's right-hand-drive rule means only factory RHD models take the routine path to registration.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty:** 0% on this lane — New Zealand's Tariff leaves most goods duty-free, with GST still payable
- **GST:** 15% on the import value — the only consumption tax at the border, and recoverable for GST-registered businesses
- **Countervailing duty:** none — New Zealand has no EU-style anti-subsidy tariff; the same BYD Seal carries a +79.9% premium into Germany once the EU's 17% CVD stacks on
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 → **$33,766.50 landed (+36.8%)**; the same car in Australia: $33,009 (+33.7%)
- **RUC:** since 1 April 2024, BEVs pay **NZ$76.00 per 1,000 km** and plug-in hybrids **NZ$38.00** (initially proposed at $53); admin fee $12.44 online or $13.71 at an agent
- **Used imports:** allowed and mainstream — every used vehicle clears a border inspection and entry certification before registration; left-hand drive is handled as a non-routine category
- **Clean Car Discount:** scrapped with effect from 31 December 2023 — no consumer rebate or fee has applied since 1 January 2024
- **The real filter:** factory RHD availability first, then the GST, then the RUC line in the running-cost math

## The terms that decide a New Zealand import

**GST (Goods and Services Tax).** New Zealand's 15% consumption tax, charged at the border on the duty-paid import value. Because the duty here is zero, it is a clean 15% on the vehicle's value.

**Landed cost.** Base price ex-factory China, plus duty, countervailing duty, GST, and fixed costs (freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland transport). Our [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/) documents the formula; every figure in this article comes from it.

**RUC (Road User Charges).** New Zealand's distance-based road tax for vehicles that don't pay fuel excise duty at the pump. You prepay in 1,000 km units and display the licence in the windscreen. Light EVs were exempt from 2009 until 1 April 2024 — and that exemption is gone.

**RHD (right-hand drive).** New Zealand drives on the left, and the routine import path runs on right-hand-drive cars. Left-hand drive vehicles are handled as a non-routine import category, with extra conditions.

**Entry certification.** The NZTA requirement that an imported vehicle be certified by an approved organisation before it can be registered and licensed for New Zealand roads. It sits after a border inspection and before your number plate.

**Clean Car Discount / Clean Car Standard.** Two different things, often conflated. The Discount — the consumer rebate-and-fee scheme — ended on 31 December 2023. The Standard is the separate part of the Clean Car Programme that NZTA administers and that applies to vehicles entering the fleet.

## The tax stack: 0% duty, 15% GST, no countervailing duty

New Zealand's border position is one of the simplest in our 30-market dataset. NZ Customs describes the Tariff plainly: most goods attract no duty at all, GST remains payable, and only some goods carry 5% or 10%. For the import lane we track, the standard duty is 0% and there is no countervailing duty — the brand-specific 17–35.3% CVD bands that dominate European landed costs do not exist here.

That leaves GST as the single variable. At 15%, applied to the duty-paid value, it is the entire statutory tax on a Chinese EV import. The comparison with Australia — the other RHD market in Oceania — is instructive:

| Line | New Zealand | Australia |
|---|---|---|
| Standard import duty | 0% | 0% |
| Countervailing duty | 0% | 0% |
| Consumption tax | GST 15% | GST 10% |
| Luxury car tax | None | 33% above the LCT threshold (higher threshold for fuel-efficient vehicles) |
| Registration fee (model input) | $23 | $500 |
| BYD Seal, landed | $33,766.50 (+36.8%) | $33,009 (+33.7%) |

The Australian side is not just trivia: Australia charges a Luxury Car Tax on the value above a threshold, with a higher threshold for fuel-efficient cars. New Zealand has no equivalent — for a high-value import like a Zeekr 001, that structural difference matters more than the five-point GST gap. [More on the Australian market here](/blog/australia-import-zero-duty/).

Two patterns are worth naming. First, the percentage premium shrinks as the car gets more expensive — fixed costs dominate cheap cars, exactly as they do in every market we track. Second, New Zealand and Australia cross over at about $9,500 ex-factory: below that line, New Zealand's near-zero registration fee outweighs its five-point-higher GST (the Seagull lands $30 cheaper in NZ); above it, Australia pulls ahead. A market where the cheaper half of the lineup is cheaper — that is unusual, and it exists purely because of the registration-fee gap.

## Worked example: a BYD Seal in New Zealand

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690.00 |
| Import duty (0%) | $0 |
| GST (15% of the import value) | $3,703.50 |
| Fixed costs — Oceania basket (freight, clearance, certification, inland) | $5,350 |
| Registration fee | $23 |
| **Total landed** | **$33,766.50** |

Formula: `base × (1 + 0.15) + 5,373`. Every input is from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 2026-09-15.

Only five markets in our dataset land the Seal more cheaply than New Zealand — Qatar, Thailand, the UAE, Australia and Malaysia. The same car lands at $45,729 in Turkey and $44,413 in Germany, and $38,112 in Norway. The caveat is what happens after the border, which is where this market changed in 2024.

## The RUC change: light EVs stopped being a road-tax-free zone on 1 April 2024

For fifteen years, New Zealand's electric vehicles paid nothing toward road upkeep beyond registration. That ended on **1 April 2024**, when light EVs and plug-in hybrids were brought into the Road User Charges system. The government's rationale was simple: the exemption had always been designed to end once EVs reached around 2% of the light vehicle fleet — and by the time it closed, there were around 100,000 light EVs on New Zealand roads.

Current rates, per NZTA:

| Vehicle type | RUC rate |
|---|---|
| Battery electric (BEV) | NZ$76.00 per 1,000 km |
| Plug-in petrol hybrid (PHEV) | NZ$38.00 per 1,000 km |
| Plug-in diesel hybrid | NZ$76.00 per 1,000 km |
| Petrol hybrid (can't plug in) | None — fuel excise is paid at the pump |
| Very light electric (under 1,000 kg, e.g. mopeds) | None |

One admin fee applies per licence purchase: $12.44 online or in the NZTA app, $13.71 at an agent. Three details for planning:

1. **The rate tracks light diesel.** NZ$76 per 1,000 km is the same rate a light diesel vehicle pays — that is the policy signature: EVs now pay the diesel-parity rate rather than nothing.
2. **The PHEV number changed before it started.** The rate was proposed at $53, cut to $38 during the select committee stage of the enabling legislation, and the final law applied $38. In plain terms: these rates are a live policy variable, not a fixed parameter. Re-check them before building any multi-year business case.
3. **The weight band and the horizon.** RUC applies to light electric vehicles from 1,001 kg to 3,500 kg GVM. Heavy electric vehicles — over 3,500 kg — join on 1 July 2027.

What does it cost in practice? NZ$76 per 1,000 km is **7.6 cents per kilometre**. A car covering 12,000 km a year pays **NZ$912 a year** — where in 2023 it paid nothing. For a plug-in hybrid at $38, the same distance costs $456, on top of the petrol it burns. This is the most under-modelled line in Chinese-EV import economics, because it does not appear in any border quote: the landed cost is a snapshot, and RUC is an annuity you pay from the odometer. For fleet TCO comparisons, add it before you compare a New Zealand import against anything.

## Right-hand drive: the filter that runs before price

Before any of the numbers above matter, the steering wheel settles the question. New Zealand drives on the left; left-hand drive vehicles are treated as a non-routine import category rather than the routine path. China's domestic market is left-hand drive, so a China-market EV cannot simply be shipped over and registered — the practical route is the factory right-hand-drive export variant. Many Chinese brands build RHD versions for exactly these markets, though they remain a subset of each lineup. [This filter applies across the whole RHD map](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) — New Zealand and Australia are the two right-hand-drive markets in Oceania, and the brands that serve one usually serve the other with the same export variants.

So the first question for any Chinese model is not "what does it cost to land" but "does an RHD export version exist". If the answer is no, the landed-cost table is academic.

## Used imports: allowed, mainstream — and not a shortcut for China-market cars

New Zealand is unusual among developed markets for how mainstream used imports are, and the NZTA publishes dedicated guidance for them. Any used import — whether newly purchased overseas or arriving as personal effects — must satisfy three agencies: **NZTA** (vehicle standards, border inspection, entry certification, registration), **MPI** (biosecurity clearance, with an approved system for vehicles exported from Japan), and **Customs** (entry, duty and GST collection, and checks for odometer tampering).

The used channel is also where an important limitation lives. New Zealand's used-import culture was built on right-hand-drive cars — historically Japanese-market vehicles — and the steering-wheel rule cuts the same way here as it does for new cars. A used China-market EV is left-hand drive, which puts it in the non-routine category; the straightforward used import is an RHD car that was first sold in a left-traffic market. If you are sourcing used Chinese EVs for New Zealand, the realistic supply is RHD units from New Zealand, Australia, the UK, or Japan — not from the China domestic fleet. One more change to keep in mind: the Clean Car Discount's used-vehicle rebate disappeared with the scheme at the end of 2023, so the used math no longer includes a subsidy.

## What buyers should check before committing

1. **Factory RHD?** If the model isn't built in right-hand drive for export, the process ends here.
2. **Which GVM band?** Under 1,000 kg: no RUC. Between 1,001 and 3,500 kg: the standard light-EV rates above. Over 3,500 kg: the 2027 change applies.
3. **What is the annual RUC bill?** NZ$76 × thousands of kilometres per year. 12,000 km → NZ$912. Build it into TCO, not into the landed price.
4. **New or used, and which compliance path?** Border inspection and entry certification apply to used imports; budget both the cost (our model carries certification inside the fixed basket) and the time.
5. **Is your GST recoverable?** For a GST-registered business the 15% is a cash-flow item, not a final cost — which is why B2B importers and private buyers should read the same table differently. The duty line — here zero — is the only genuinely permanent border cost.
6. **Does your car's charging port match New Zealand's network?** New Zealand's public charging runs on the Type 2/CCS2 standard; a China-market car charges on GB/T. [The adapter and conversion question is here](/blog/ccs2-vs-gbt-charging/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Does New Zealand charge import duty on Chinese EVs?**
No. Our tariff records show a 0% standard duty for this lane, and NZ Customs notes most goods in the Tariff are duty-free, with GST still payable. There is no countervailing duty of the kind the EU applies.

**How much GST will I pay?**
15% of the import value. On a $24,690 BYD Seal that is $3,703.50 — and if you are GST-registered, it is recoverable.

**What are road user charges, and how much are they?**
A prepaid, distance-based road tax for vehicles that don't pay fuel excise at source. Since 1 April 2024, light BEVs pay NZ$76.00 per 1,000 km and plug-in hybrids NZ$38.00, with a $12.44 admin fee online or $13.71 at an agent.

**Can I import a used Chinese EV to New Zealand?**
Used imports are allowed and mainstream, but they go through border inspection and entry certification. Because China-market cars are left-hand drive and the routine path is right-hand drive, the straightforward used flow is RHD cars from left-traffic markets such as Japan, the UK, or Australia.

**Is New Zealand cheaper than Australia for a Chinese EV?**
It depends on the price point. In our model the two markets cross at about $9,500 ex-factory: below that, New Zealand's $23 registration fee beats Australia's $500 by more than the GST difference; above it, Australia's 10% GST wins. The Seal: $33,766.50 in NZ versus $33,009 in Australia.

**Do Chinese EVs get any purchase subsidy in New Zealand now?**
No. The last consumer scheme ended on 31 December 2023 and nothing has replaced it.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, New Zealand is the clearest example on the Chinese-EV export map of a market where the tariff is a non-event and the running cost is the story — and anyone still modelling it with the pre-2024 "EVs pay nothing" assumption is modelling a market that no longer exists.

**Reasoning.** First, the border math is genuinely open — 0% duty, no countervailing duty, no luxury car tax — but the 15% GST is the whole statutory tax, and it is flat: there is nothing to structure around unless you are GST-registered. Second, the RUC change is the most under-modelled line in this market's economics: NZ$76 per 1,000 km is the light-diesel rate, and at 12,000 km a year it is NZ$912 that simply did not exist before April 2024 — a running-cost variable that matters more than every tariff line here combined, precisely because there are no tariff lines. Third, the RHD filter and entry certification decide the shortlist before price does, and they also quietly exclude the used-import route most people assume exists, because China-market cars are left-hand drive.

**Disclosure.** This is my analysis and opinion, not verified fact, and not legal or tax advice. Rates and schemes in this market have moved repeatedly since 2023 — RUC rates, rebates, and thresholds should be confirmed with NZTA or a customs broker before you commit capital.
