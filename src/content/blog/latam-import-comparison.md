## TL;DR

Latin America's two biggest auto markets finished building their tariff walls within six months of each other — and built them differently. Mexico charges a flat **50% import duty plus 16% IVA** on Chinese cars since 1 January 2026, with no EV carve-out and no countervailing duty; Brazil reached **35% import tax (II) plus a 17% ICMS-equivalent layer** on 1 July 2026 at the end of a three-year ramp. On EV Hub's records a [BYD Seal](/vehicles/byd-seal/) lands at **$50,011 in Mexico (+102.6%)** and **$45,568 in Brazil (+84.6%)** — above the same car's EU-stack landing in Germany ($44,413). The gap is exact: **Mexico − Brazil = 16.05% of the China base plus $480**. Brazil's column is a floor, not a ceiling.

## Key statistics

- **Mexico:** 50% duty (up from 20%, effective 1 January 2026) + 16% IVA; no countervailing duty; no EV exemption — petrol, diesel and battery cars all pay the top rate
- **Brazil:** 35% BEV import tax from 1 July 2026 (ramp: 10% → 18% → 25% → 35%); a ~17% ICMS-equivalent VAT layer in our model; no countervailing duty
- **Fixed import block:** $7,050 Mexico · $6,570 Brazil (freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland)
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 ex-factory → **$50,011 Mexico · $45,568 Brazil**
- **The gap:** Mexico − Brazil = **16.05% of base + $480** — exact on every model, because both tax multipliers are fixed
- **Demand side:** Chinese brands took ~17% of Mexico's light-vehicle market in H1 2026 despite the live tariff; Brazilian EV imports keep declining duty-free quotas through 2027
- **FX (22 September 2026):** USD/MXN 17.25 · USD/BRL 5.12

## The two stacks, side by side

| Layer | Mexico | Brazil |
|---|---|---|
| Import duty | **50%** (all powertrains, since 1 Jan 2026) | **35%** (BEV II, since 1 Jul 2026) |
| VAT layer | **16% IVA** on the duty-paid value | **17% ICMS-equivalent** in our model; real ICMS is state-level and grossed up |
| Countervailing duty | None | None |
| Layers our model excludes | None — IVA is the last tax | **IPI, PIS-Import, COFINS-Import, AFRMM** — the real bill is higher |
| Fixed block (our records) | $7,050 | $6,570 |
| Escape route | Local assembly (not an import) | Local assembly; zero-tariff CKD/SKD quota (~USD 463m, H2 2026) |

Mexico's stack is short and complete: duty, then IVA, and that is the whole bill — which is why our [Mexico guide](/blog/mexico-import-guide/) states the landed figure as exact. Brazil's stack is long, and our model deliberately counts only one VAT-equivalent layer; the full chain — IPI on the customs value plus II, then PIS/COFINS-Import, then ICMS grossed up on a base that already contains the other taxes — is documented in our [Brazil guide](/blog/brazil-import-guide/) and makes $45,568 the floor of a range. That asymmetry is the most important thing about comparing the two numbers.

Three models spanning the catalog, landed through both markets on our model. Base price is ex-factory China; totals include duty, the VAT layer and the fixed import block.

|---|---|---|---|---|

Brazil wins every row. The premium percentages invert the ranking as prices fall — Mexico runs from +117.4% on the Atto 3 down to +93.7% on the YU7 — because the fixed import block weighs on cheap cars proportionally harder, as it does in the [Nordics](/blog/nordics-import-comparison/) and [ASEAN](/blog/asean-import-comparison/) comparisons. Compare absolute dollars, not percentages.

## What the gap contains

The two columns decompose into an exact formula, because both multipliers and both fixed blocks are constants:
`Mexico − Brazil = base × (1.50 × 1.16 − 1.35 × 1.17) + ($7,050 − $6,570) = 16.05% of base + $480`

On the Seal that is $3,962.75 + $480 = $4,442.75; the same coefficients reproduce every row of the seven-model grids in our two market guides. The 15-point duty gap does the heavy lifting; Brazil's one-point-higher VAT layer takes back only a sliver. The fixed block is the residual between the published formula and the published total in each vehicle record, bundling RoRo freight, clearance, certification, registration and inland transport.

### Worked example: a BYD Seal through both walls

| Line | Mexico | Brazil |
|---|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690 | $24,690 |
| Import duty | $12,345 (50%) | $8,642 (35% II) |
| VAT layer | $5,926 (16% IVA) | $5,666 (17% ICMS-equivalent) |
| Fixed import block | $7,050 | $6,570 |
| **Total landed** | **$50,011** | **$45,568** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Inputs from EV Hub's tariff and vehicle records dated 15 September 2026.

One caveat that reverses part of the comparison: the Mexico column is the complete bill by construction — nothing sits between the duty and the IVA. The Brazil column stops before IPI, PIS-Import, COFINS-Import and AFRMM, which sit between the II and the ICMS in a real entry. The effective gap is narrower than $4,443 on the Seal — possibly much narrower on premium models — and only Mexico's number can be quoted without a range.

## The policy engines behind the walls

Mexico's 50% was an industrial-policy answer to trade pressure, not an EV tax. The Senate approved the package in December 2025, effective 1 January 2026, covering 1,400-plus product lines (the Secretaría de Economía's count is 1,463 tariff fractions) — and it applies only to countries without a free-trade agreement with Mexico, which in practice means China: USMCA-built cars enter duty-free. Washington had been explicit that it saw Chinese plants in Mexico as a back door into the US market, and BYD and MG were already establishing Mexican operations. The wall is Mexico's answer to the jump-pad thesis — ship to Mexico, sell into North America — which our [Mexico guide](/blog/mexico-import-guide/) argues is dead at these rates. The market has not collapsed: Chinese brands held roughly 17% of light-vehicle sales in the first half of 2026, but that share ran on pre-tariff inventory, and Chinese light-vehicle imports fell about 43% by value in the first five months of the year.

Brazil's 35% arrived by schedule instead of decree. The ramp — 10% in January 2024, 18% in July 2024, 25% in July 2025, 35% in July 2026 — was announced in advance, hits the Mercosur ceiling, and its revenue funds the Mover Program, the green-mobility incentive that pays for domestic production. Declining duty-free quotas run through 2027, but the preferential CKD/SKD assembly-kit rate has been withdrawn, reaching parity with the CBU rate by 1 January 2027, with a zero-tariff kit quota (roughly USD 463 million) for the second half of 2026 as the remaining valve. Brazil is also mid-tax-reform: the CBS/IBS dual VAT is scheduled to replace IPI, PIS, COFINS, ICMS and ISS by 2033. Both walls say the same thing in different accents: **assemble here, or pay at the border**.

## Which route is cheaper: the decision framework

1. **On pure CBU border cost, Brazil wins** — by 16.05% of base plus $480 on every model, before counting the federal layers our model excludes.
2. **On certainty, Mexico wins** — duty plus IVA is the entire bill; there is no hidden cascade. Brazil's number needs a full NCM-level entry simulation (II, IPI, PIS, COFINS, ICMS, AFRMM), and the ICMS rate varies by state.
3. **On market logic, they buy different things** — Mexico is a North American production platform with the US watching its Chinese plants; Brazil is a Mercosur-scale domestic market that funds its own EV industry with your tariff.4. **Both markets now price assembly as the business model.** Without a local-assembly path, budget the wall; with one, budget the investment.

## The Author's Take

### Position

In my view the honest summary of these two walls is: **Brazil is cheaper on paper, Mexico is cheaper to plan against, and both are expensive enough to force local assembly.** Anyone quoting 50% against 35% as if the difference were 15 points is doing the wrong arithmetic — and anyone treating the Brazil number as final is doing worse.
### Reasoning

First, the arithmetic is exact: the Mexico−Brazil gap is 16.05% of base plus $480 on every model, so Brazil's edge grows with price — $3,090 on an Atto 3, $6,210 on a YU7. Second, the edge is overstated by our own model: Mexico's column is the complete bill, while Brazil's excludes IPI, PIS/COFINS and the ICMS gross-up that our [Brazil guide](/blog/brazil-import-guide/) treats as the real policy event of 2026. Third, the direction of travel is identical — Mexico's tariff targets exactly the Chinese import channel, Brazil's funds a local-production program while closing the kit loophole — so the decision converges on one question: assemble where? Fourth, a 17% Mexican share under a live 50% wall shows the market absorbs the price for now, but the roughly 43% import-value collapse shows the channel is already re-pricing. I would model both markets, but quote only one without caveats — Mexico's.
