## TL;DR

Importing a Chinese EV to the Netherlands means paying the full EU customs stack — **10% standard duty, a brand-specific countervailing duty of 17% to 35.3%, and 21% VAT** on the running total — in the market whose port of Rotterdam makes it a primary deep-sea gateway for the trade. On our model a [BYD Seal](/vehicles/byd-seal/) lands at about **$44,949 (+82.1%)**: exactly **$250 above Belgium** and **$536 above Germany**, with the gap coming from the registration line and VAT — never from the duty, which is set once for the whole EU.

## Key statistics

- **Import duty:** 10% — the EU's common external tariff on electric passenger cars (CN 8703 80 10), identical at every EU border
- **Countervailing duty (CVD):** brand-specific — 17.0% BYD group (incl. Denza and Yangwang) · 18.8% Geely group (Geely, Zeekr, Lynk & Co) · 20.7% most other cooperating producers (the default tier — Neta, Xiaomi and others) · 35.3% SAIC brands (MG, Maxus, Wuling, Baojun) · 7.8% Tesla Shanghai — battery-electric cars only, in force since 30 October 2024
- **VAT:** 21% on the duty-paid value — higher than Germany's 19% and France's 20%, level with Belgium's
- **Registration:** $400 flat in our records — between Belgium's $150 and Germany's $500; the bpm-specific computation is outside our dataset (see below)
- **Worked example:** BYD Seal $24,690 ex-factory → **$44,949 landed (+82.1%)**
- **Netherlands vs Belgium:** +$250 on all eight models we ran — exactly the registration-line difference, because both charge 21% VAT on the same duty-paid base
- **Netherlands vs Germany:** +$49 (Wuling Hongguang MINI EV) to +$1,516 (Zeekr 009) — a two-point VAT gap partly offset by Germany's higher registration line
- **Catalogue range:** across the 517 models with a Netherlands landing in our records, premiums run from +36.4% (Hongqi Guoya) to +246.4% (Bestune Pony)
- **Records basis:** EV Hub tariff, market and vehicle records (30 markets), last verified 15 September 2026; tariff basis EU Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754

## The terms that decide this market

- **Landed cost** — the importer-side cost to bring a China-market car to a Dutch buyer, registered and compliant. Base price → import duty → countervailing duty → VAT → freight → clearance → certification → registration → inland transport. The full formula is documented in our [landed-cost methodology](/docs/landed-cost-methodology/).
- **MFN duty** — the EU's "most-favoured-nation" tariff of 10% that applies to cars from any country without a free trade agreement covering them. China has no EU car FTA, so 10% is the floor at every entry point from Rotterdam to Antwerp to Hamburg.
- **Countervailing duty (CVD)** — an anti-subsidy tariff layered on top of the MFN duty. For Chinese BEVs, the EU set brand-specific rates in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754, in force since 30 October 2024 in our records, and it applies to battery-electric cars only — plug-in hybrids and range-extenders sit outside its scope.
- **CIF** — cost, insurance, freight; the customs value. In our model duty applies to the ex-factory value, with freight, clearance and certification carried as a fixed block per market.
- **Compounding, not adding** — the stack multiplies. Duty comes first, then the CVD is calculated on the duty-paid value, then VAT on the running total. Never sum 10 + 35.3 + 21 = 66.3%; only the line-item math gets it right.
- **One customs territory** — the Netherlands sits inside the EU customs union, so the duty and CVD bill is identical whether the car clears in Rotterdam, Antwerp or Hamburg. Inside the EU, only VAT, registration fees and local taxes move the number — which is why the Netherlands-versus-Belgium gap in this article is $250, not $2,500.
- **bpm** — the Netherlands' registration-stage vehicle tax. Our market records carry a flat $400 registration line and do not document the bpm's computation, exemptions, or how zero-emission imports are treated under it. That detail could not be verified from our sources — treat it as UNKNOWN and confirm with the Belastingdienst or a licensed customs broker.
- **Rotterdam, the gateway** — the Netherlands' deep-sea port and one of the standard northern-European landing points for RoRo car carriers arriving from Asia. What it changes is logistics — terminal, broker and distribution depth — not the tariff math, which is continental. More on that below.

## What a Chinese EV pays at the Dutch border

| Layer | Rate for a China-origin BEV | Levied on | In our landed model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Import duty (MFN) | **10%** | CIF value | Yes |
| Countervailing duty | **17% BYD group / 18.8% Geely group / 20.7% default / 35.3% SAIC / 7.8% Tesla Shanghai** | duty-paid value | Yes |
| VAT | **21%** | running total (value + duty + CVD) | Yes |
| Registration & plates | **$400 flat in our records** — bpm computation not documented in our dataset (UNKNOWN) | — | Yes |

Read the table top to bottom and the structure is familiar from every EU market: a customs layer that is the heaviest single block in the stack, and a Dutch registration line our model keeps deliberately flat because the underlying tax could not be verified from our sources. What differs inside the EU is small print — 21% VAT here against Germany's 19%, a $400 registration line against Belgium's $150 — and that small print is where the Netherlands' price position is decided. The bloc-level mechanics get the full treatment in our [Germany vs Netherlands vs France comparison](/blog/germany-netherlands-france-import-comparison/).

Two patterns jump out, and both are structural.

First, **the CVD tier on the badge sets your border bill more than the car's spec sheet does.** The [MG 4](/vehicles/mg-4/) and the [Neta V](/vehicles/neta-v/) start $1,370 apart ex-factory, yet the MG lands $4,084 higher — because SAIC's 35.3% rate applies to MG while Neta sits at the 20.7% default tier. Put the same price tag on both tiers and the gap is starker still: at an identical $25,000 ex-factory, a 35.3% SAIC car and a 17% BYD car land roughly **$6,100 apart** once duty and VAT compound on the CVD difference. That spread is set in Brussels, not The Hague.

Second, **cheap cars wear the fixed block worst.** A $5,000 Wuling pays the same freight, clearance, certification, registration and inland block as a $61,830 Zeekr 009 — $6,500 in our model, of which $3,250 is compliance and certification alone — so its percentage premium runs to +210.1% while the Zeekr 009 "only" pays +68.6%. Percentage comparisons between a microcar and a luxury MPV tell you almost nothing about either import decision. Absolute landed cost and incremental tax do.

### Worked example: a BYD Seal in the Netherlands

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Base price (ex-factory China) | $24,690.00 |
| Import duty (10% of base) | $2,469.00 |
| Countervailing duty (17% of base + duty) | $4,617.03 |
| VAT (21% of base + duty + CVD) | $6,672.97 |
| Freight, clearance, certification, registration, inland | $6,500.00 |
| **Total landed** | **$44,949.00** |

Formula: `base × (1 + duty) × (1 + CVD) × (1 + VAT) + fixed costs`. Every input comes from EV Hub's tariff and landed-cost records dated 15 September 2026.

One caution on the registration line: the model folds registration into a flat $400 for comparability, but the Netherlands' actual bpm treatment of an imported zero-emission car could not be verified from our sources — treat the exact registration computation as UNKNOWN and confirm it with the Belastingdienst or a licensed customs broker before committing. The $44,949 figure is a planning floor, not a showroom estimate.

| Zeekr 009 | $104,267 | +68.6% | $104,017 | $102,751 | +$250 | +$1,516 |

Two things to read from this table. First, **the Netherlands–Belgium gap is exactly $250 on every model** — the difference between a $400 and a $150 registration line and nothing else, because both countries charge 21% VAT on the same duty-paid base. Second, **the Netherlands–Germany gap is a two-part wash**: Germany's 19% VAT is worth two points of the duty-paid value in its favour — about $636 on the Seal — while its $500 registration line gives back $100; the net is the $536 difference you see, and it scales with the car's value down the table.

[Belgium's own guide](/blog/belgium-import-guide/) frames the same numbers from the other side: the same car, $250 cheaper, landing through Antwerp-Zeebrugge. [Sweden](/blog/sweden-import-guide/) sits above all three at 25% VAT. None of this changes at the port, though — the [LHD vs RHD map](/blog/lhd-vs-rhd-markets/) matters more than the harbour when you are choosing where a car can ultimately be sold.

## Why the Netherlands still matters: the Rotterdam gateway

In our records, the Netherlands is one of the EU's core landing markets for the CVD-era trade, and its pull is not only demand. It is geography. Rotterdam is the country's deep-sea port and one of the standard northern-European landing points for the RoRo car carriers that move China-built cars into Europe; the Dutch corridor then feeds inventory across the single market.

For an importer, two things follow. First, the arithmetic above does not change at the harbour. Inside the EU customs union, clearing in Rotterdam, Antwerp or Hamburg produces the same duty and CVD bill — the mirror image of the [Belgium guide's](/blog/belgium-import-guide/) one-customs-territory point. The port you pick moves your logistics cost and paperwork, never your tariff line. Second, capability concentrates at exactly these gateway ports — terminal handling, broker depth, PDI and distribution — which is why "import into the Netherlands" is, operationally, as close to a standard procedure as this trade offers.

What our dataset does not carry is Rotterdam's vehicle throughput — port volume statistics sit outside our records, so treat any specific figure as UNKNOWN and go to the port authority's releases for it. The part this article prices is what a car owes once it lands, and that is the stack above: unchanged by the quay it crosses.

## What importers should check before committing

1. **Which CVD tier does your brand sit in?** The difference between 17% and 35.3% on a $25,000 car compounds to about $6,100 in the Netherlands once 21% VAT is applied. Confirm the rate for the exact exporting producer, not just the badge — and note the rates here reflect our dataset as of 15 September 2026; any later EU-level developments could not be verified for this article (UNKNOWN).
2. **Budget the fixed block honestly.** $6,500 in our Dutch model — freight $2,000, clearance $350, certification $3,250, registration $400, inland $500. It does not scale down with the car, which is why cheap models show the worst percentages.
3. **Get the registration position in writing.** Our $400 line is a model input; the bpm computation and any treatment specific to zero-emission imports could not be verified from our sources (UNKNOWN). Confirm with the Belastingdienst or a Dutch broker before sale.
4. **Clarify VAT recovery for your structure.** Whether the 21% import VAT is recoverable depends on the buyer and the use of the vehicle; our records capture the rate, not the recovery rules (UNKNOWN). One email to the Belastingdienst settles it before, not after, you ship.
5. **Check the homologation route.** A car with EU whole-vehicle type approval can be registered without extra technical documentation; a China-only homologation needs a single-vehicle procedure — our [WVTA vs single-vehicle approval](/blog/wvta-vs-single-vehicle-approval/) explainer covers the difference.
6. **Left-hand drive and CCS2.** The Netherlands is a left-hand-drive market on CCS2 charging; a China-market car needs the right charging configuration rather than a steering conversion — our [CCS2 vs GB/T note](/blog/ccs2-vs-gbt-charging/) covers the compatibility cost.
7. **Plan the entry port for logistics, not for tax.** Rotterdam, Antwerp and Hamburg produce the same customs bill; choose on freight rates, transit time and broker capability, and expect the duty to be identical wherever the car lands.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** In my view, the Netherlands is the cleanest illustration in Europe that the customs story is continental while the price story is national: the same Chinese EV carries an identical duty and CVD bill at every EU border, and yet lands $250 above Belgium and $536 above Germany — purely on a registration line and a VAT rate. The Dutch case carries a second layer most market guides skip: this is where the cars physically arrive, through Rotterdam, and the gateway's real value is operational depth, not tariff arbitrage.

**Reasoning.** First, the mechanism is fully visible in our records: identical 10% duty and identical brand CVDs across the three markets, a 21% VAT rate the Netherlands shares with Belgium, and a registration line ($400) that sits between Belgium's ($150) and Germany's ($500). The $250 Netherlands–Belgium gap is exactly the registration difference — nothing else — and it holds on every one of the eight models we ran. Second, the direction of the Netherlands–Germany gap depends on the car: a two-point VAT penalty that grows with value, partly offset by a $100 registration advantage, running from +$49 on a Wuling to +$1,516 on a Zeekr 009. Third, the catalogue-wide premium band — +36.4% to +246.4% across 517 models — shows how much of the outcome is the car's price class rather than the tariff itself: a cheap car pays the same $6,500 fixed block as an expensive one. Fourth, the policy clock is worth watching: the CVD regime in our records dates from late 2024, and any later EU-level developments around it could not be verified for this article (UNKNOWN) — treat brand-level rates as the current state, not a permanent one.

**Disclosure.** This is my analysis of published rules, tariff records and market data — not legal, tax or customs advice, and not a quote.
